# 4. The look: 3D world and 2D maps

The site document from the generator is turned into two kinds of visual asset: a 3D world to walk through in the browser, and 2D maps. This document defines the target for both and the approach to reaching it.

## Target

A first-person 3D world in the browser with physically based materials, normal-mapped surfaces and lighting good enough that screenshots are worth taking. Grounded and believable in its materials, heightened in its lighting and colour.

The standard to hold: **a generated place should not look generated.** No visible grid, no visibly repeating texture, no uniform lighting, no flat walls meeting at perfect corners.

## Principles

1. **Light first.** Lighting is the largest single contributor to how good a scene looks, and the one most often left as an afterthought in generated worlds. It gets its own pipeline layer and its own budget.
2. **Form before surface.** Normal maps add grain, not shape. This is not a low-poly look: every stone is its own piece of geometry with a bevelled edge and a recessed mortar joint, sitting slightly proud or shy of its neighbours. Walls get plinths and cornices; rubble gathers at the foot of a ruin. Anything that should cast a shadow or catch a highlight on its edge is modelled. A modest courtyard and hall comes to several thousand stones and several hundred thousand triangles, which current hardware draws easily.
3. **Fields drive surfaces.** The history layer already knows where it is damp, old, hot, overgrown and well-trodden. Those fields choose and blend materials, which is what ties the look to the place's story.
4. **Hide the machine.** Break up tiling, vary instances, jitter what would be regular, and never let the underlying grid show.
5. **One document, many views.** The 3D world and every map style are rendered from the same data and always agree.

## Geometry: from spans to meshes

The span grid is logical. Separate builders turn it into visible geometry, selected by each zone's origin.

| Builder | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **Terrain** | Open-air spans, material weights | Continuous ground surface with level of detail; cliffs given real rock faces rather than stretched slopes |
| **Architecture** | Built spans, storeys, openings | Walls with thickness, floors, vaults and ceilings, stairs, roofs derived from footprints |
| **Natural rock** | A function giving how far inside open air each point is | Smooth irregular surfaces extracted with marching cubes, so caves have curves and overhangs. Built: see below |
| **Kit of parts** | Sockets from archetypes and openings | Arches, columns, door and window frames, battlements, beams, railings |
| **Vegetation** | Growth field, flora rules | Trees grown procedurally, undergrowth and ground cover as instances, ivy and roots following surfaces |
| **Ruin** | Decay field, events | Broken wall tops, missing roofs, fallen blocks, rubble |
| **Water** | Water level and flow from history | Surfaces for pools, rivers and flooded spans |

The kit of parts is itself procedural and parameterised by builder culture, so one arch recipe yields a pointed arch for one culture and a round one for another. Seams between builders (a cave breaking into a cellar, a wall founded on rock) are handled explicitly, since those borders are where sites are most interesting.

### Natural rock

Where masonry is laid stone by stone on a surface, rock is carved. A cave is described by a function that is positive in open air and negative in rock: an uneven dome over a rough oval floor, with a bay reaching towards each doorway, roughened by noise at two scales. The surface between air and rock is extracted with marching cubes at about a quarter of a metre.

- **Texture without stretching.** Each triangle takes its texture from the direction it mostly faces: floor and ceiling from above, walls from the side. Triangles do not share vertices, so neighbouring triangles can differ. Shading stays smooth because normals come from the function itself.
- **Winding from the tables, never from the slope.** Where two parts of the function meet in a crease, its slope can point the wrong way. A triangle wound by the slope then faces away and leaves a hole to the sky. Winding is fixed by the marching cubes tables.
- **Closed by construction.** Whatever the noise does, the rock is forced solid before it reaches the edge of the volume being carved, except towards a masonry wall, which hides the edge.
- **Where rock meets masonry.** A cave has masonry only on the side where the builders walled it off to hang a door. That wall is laid as usual; the rock runs a little way past its plane and the wall hides the join.

Rock strata are tinted per vertex in tilted beds. Caves are unlit: they are places for the carried light.

### Cliffs, boulders and scree

Out of doors the rock is made in three steps, each of which the next depends on.

1. **Beds.** Each kind of stone has a character: how thick its beds are, how far apart its upright joints, how much one bed differs from the next, whether it weathers round or breaks sharp. Limestone is thick level beds in blocks; sandstone thin beds of very different hardness, worn round; greystone massive and angular; basalt close upright joints. Beds dip a little and roll, and come in runs: a few hard ones together, then a few soft.
2. **Wearing.** The wall round the valley starts as a smooth rise, steeper than rock would stand. It is then worn on a lattice of heights the way frost wears a cliff: wherever rock stands steeper than its bed can hold, some comes away and lands on the lowest ground beside it; wherever that debris lies steeper than it can, it slides on. Soft beds fall back into ledges, hard ones are left as faces, and scree gathers at the foot. Most of what falls is taken to have weathered away, or the scree would bury the cliff. The lowest four or five metres are always sound rock, so the wall begins with a face too high to scramble up.
3. **Carving.** The worn heights are carved as a solid on a lattice of 30 centimetres, in a band round the valley and across its head, in a way that keeps edges sharp (below). Where the ground is steep and bare, the surface is moved out or back from where the heights put it by the rock's relief: soft beds cut back under hard ones; each step of a few beds brought upright, so the face goes up in treads and risers; the beds split into blocks by joints (cells in three dimensions, kept in layers), each block standing a little proud or shy and a few missing; broad buttresses and gullies; and, in stone that breaks sharp, flat faces meeting in edges. The face is moved sideways, never up and down: that is what lets a bed overhang the one below, and keeps the top of a cliff from breaking into teeth.

The meadow before the door and the scarp either side of it are carved the same way from the same lattice and baked with the house; the rest of the rim is not baked, and takes its light from the open sky, dimmed in cracks and under overhangs by feeling for rock in front of each point. Every carved patch ends on the lines of the sheet of ground beyond it, so the two meet without a join. (The sheet's cell is 1.2 metres; `?sheet=` lays it otherwise, and the carving's lattice then divides the cell exactly, as near 30 centimetres as it can: 0.267 for a sheet of 0.8. The lattice the band is worn on stays at 30 centimetres either way.)

**Keeping edges.** Marching cubes, which carves the caves, puts the surface's points on the edges of the sampling cells and joins them, so a corner inside a cell is cut straight across and everything comes out rounded to the size of a cell. Rock in the open is carved another way (dual contouring). Each cell the surface passes through gets one point, placed where the surface's faces meet: every crossing of the cell's edges gives a spot the surface goes through and the way it faces there, each such pair is a plane, and the point is put where the planes come closest to meeting. On a flat face that is anywhere on the face; on a crease it is on the crease; at a corner it is the corner. Points of neighbouring cells are joined across every lattice edge the surface crosses. A ledge, the edge of a block or the meeting of two fracture faces then comes out as a true edge at the same lattice size and the same cost. Shading keeps them: the faces round each point are gathered into groups lying within 36 degrees of each other, and each group is shaded as one smooth surface, so a face is smooth and an edge is an edge. Pieces of land are sampled one cell beyond their own edge so that neighbours make identical points along it, and are closed exactly to the edge where they meet the plain sheet of ground. Identical means to the last bit: the rock's function has sharp breaks in it (its joints lie on a lattice of their own, every 2.6 metres in some stone), and where one fell exactly on an edge two pieces shared, a difference in the last place of each piece's own reckoning of where that edge lay put a point in rock for one and in air for the other, and left a hole through the cliff a hand or two across. So a piece counts its points from the lattice's origin and not from its own corner, and both find the same numbers. A piece is also as tall as the ground a little beyond it needs, not only its own: the relief pushes a face sideways by up to four metres, and reads heights from there. And where carving meets the sheet, the two drew their common edge each in its own way (the carving every 40 centimetres, following the ground, the sheet straight from one of its points to the next every 1.2 metres), and a sliver of sky showed between them; the carving's closing points are now put on the sheet's own straight edge (on the rim and the shelves, which end on the sheet; not the meadow and the hill over the house, which end on the house, where the sheet's height is the hilltop's and closing on it raised rock into the doorway). (A lip of sheet run on under the carving was tried first, and came up through the ground in grey shards wherever the carving lay lower than the sheet.) Last, the faces round each lattice edge are turned to face the air together, by the way the four of them face as a whole, and cut into two triangles along whichever diagonal folds the less, or round a point in their middle if both fold; where they stand nearly edge-on to their edge, as the faces that close a piece along its side do, the slope of the rock's function says which way is out. Each triangle used to be turned by its own facing, and in a sharp fold a small one stood nearly edge-on and was turned into the rock as often as not, where it is not drawn: specks of sky all over a cliff. Measured by firing a hundred thousand lines of sight from the sea at an island's cliffs and counting those whose first hit is the back of a face: 84 before, 5 after, all in front of the cliff line, among the boulders.

A boulder is a block bounded by flat faces, as rock that has split along beds and joints is: a few planes round it, two for its beds, corners knocked off, sometimes a crack right through, the beds showing as grooves round its sides, and the same faceting or rounding as the cliffs. Scree is a dozen such shapes carved once and set down by the hundred wherever the wearing left debris, with larger blocks among them; the ground under it has a texture of its own.

**The rock material.** Carved rock has no texture coordinates worth having, so its texture is laid on from three sides by position and blended by which way the surface faces: nothing stretches on any slope or under any overhang. The texture is a broken surface: flat faces at three sizes meeting in sharp edges, pits, grain, a few hairline cracks. It is read twice more: very large, for slow changes of tone, so no repeat shows; and four times enlarged for the normal map alone, which gives faces a stride across that the carved surface is too coarse to hold an edge on. On steep faces the shader draws thin beds within the thick ones and dark streaks where water runs down. Moss and damp come from per-vertex fields, as on masonry. Stone that breaks in flat faces (greystone, basalt, and a little in limestone) is also lit face by face: the plane of each face is read from how the surface slopes across the picture (screen-space derivatives), mixed a fifth of the way into the normal, and where it parts from the smooth normal an edge catches a little extra light (`uRockFacet`, set from the stone's `sharp`, squared). Both fade out between 45 and 120 metres, where a pixel covers many faces. Taken further than a fifth the carved surface's own triangles begin to show, and the texture's grain is flattened, so it is kept mild. The idea came from a prototype in `lab/rock-lab.ts` (a standalone page, not used by the game).

**Limestone, and what carving can hold.** (17 November 2026, worked out in the material lab against three pictures: the stacked grey towers of a karst, a jungle tower, and a pale massive face with a pinnacle.) Limestone in the open is water-worn, and four things make it so. *Its beds are unequal* (`uneven` in a stone's character): `Strata.at` lets the count of beds run ahead of the height and fall behind, so plates a hand thick lie under beds of two metres; the shader's thin beds do the same, and the plane between two of them is open in one place, a hair in another, shut in a third, where before every one was a ruled dark line. *Its beds are cushions*: with `round`, each bed swells between the planes above and below it, and is cut back less sharply by hardness. *It is cleft* (`worn`): upright joints some paces apart are opened into clefts from top to bottom, so a face stands in piers. *Its surface is worked* (`uRockWorn`, from `worn`): open planes are grooves with lips that catch the light, faces carry runnels as light and shade, the streaks are a grey film with rust in places, and stretches of it are massive with hardly a line. It is also much paler than it was.

*Beds at three sizes.* The material is laid in metres, so a bed is as thick on a mountain as on a boulder, which is right; what was wrong is that only one size of bed was drawn, 0.3 metres, and that is right at one distance only. On a face a hundred metres off it had either faded out or showed as a few stray lines. Now `bedsOf` in the rock material draws beds of 0.3, 1.4 and 6.5 metres, each with its own shades, unequal thickness and open and shut planes, and each for as long as it is large enough in the picture not to shimmer. A size shows its shades wherever it can be seen, but its dark lines only once the size below has grown too small to show its own, so a face seen close is not ruled across with lines its shape does not answer to. This is for every stone, in the measure its `bedded` gives.

*A mountain's face.* Three more things for rock seen from far off, found with the lab's mountain. The dark streaks down a face are read from the texture a stride or two apart, and the texture comes round every three metres: on a face a furlong off that was a fence of even upright bars, and with the beds across it a chequerboard. Beyond 30 to 100 metres the streaks are now read eight times as broad. A far face is carved too coarsely to hold a buttress or a ledge, so the normal map is read a third time, sixty metres to the repeat, coming in between 30 and 130 metres: the broken surface of a mountainside as light and shade. And the larger beds have lips that catch the light as the plates do. None of this moves the surface: a far face's outline is still what its coarse carving gives.

Two things were wrong with all carved cliffs and were found on the way. The relief asked the carving for what its samples, 0.3 metres apart, cannot hold: joints 0.14 wide, steps eased over 0.17, soft beds cut 0.8 deep into beds 0.4 thick. `cliffRelief` now takes the spacing (`grain`) and eases everything over at least a sample, and does not cut back a bed too thin to carve. And the carver felt for the slope of the face a finger's breadth to either side, where the rock's roughness points every way; so the points it placed jumped about in their cells and the face came out in splinters and odd flat triangles. Cliffs are now carved with `normalReach` of half a sample, and round stone with a wider crease angle. Whether a plane between two thin beds is open at a point along the face is read from plain noise, not from the stone's texture, which varied too little and left lines running the length of a cliff. Rock in shade is also lit by the sky as its grain turns it (it was lit as the smooth surface under the grain, and looked like flat paint), and within a few metres the normal map is read once more at a hand's scale.

**Rock at three sizes.** Stone is now carved at three scales, and one material serves all of them. *Boulders and loose rock* are carved finest (0.08 to 0.26 metres), since they are walked up to and round. *Cliffs* that bound where one can go are carved at 0.3. *Crags* are background: two to four masses of the valley's stone standing out of the nearer hills, 260 to 335 metres from its middle, each a tor forty to eighty metres high with a summit or two, carved at 1.6 metres (`crags` in the mesher, after the mountains are laid). Before them the valley's stone was never seen larger than a cliff: the hills are a sheet with a material that only suggests rock. The rock material does not know which of the three it is on; it draws beds, stains and relief at whatever size the distance to each point calls for. A valley's land takes about two seconds longer to build for its crags. Islands have none yet.

**Rock laid as a sheet.** Where ground too steep to be turf is not carved (banks beyond the carved band, the found land's own ravines, the far slopes of an island or a mountain top), it is laid with the land's sheet and drawn with `rockSheet`: the rock material with the rock of a face drawn on it per point of the picture, as on far land (`rockRelief`: gullies some twenty metres apart and ribs some seven running down the face, broken dipping ledges every ten metres or so, chips), bending the light and darkening its hollows, each size kept while it is four or five points of the picture across. Without it such a bank was one smooth face, plaster with beds painted on. How far along the face a point lies is read off the world's own axes, x for a face that looks along z and z for one that looks along x, the two blended where it turns: read along the face's own direction, which turns a little from one point of the picture to the next on a curved face thousands of metres from the middle of the world, it swung by metres, and every great wall far off was drawn in contour lines like the grain of wood.

**Where the hills begin.** The sheet of the far hills begins 215 metres from the valley's middle, at whatever height its hills have there: as much as fifty metres above the nearer ground, which someone else lays. The gap was closed by turning the sheet's edge down, and that edge was a wall round the valley's mouth: rounded pillars fifty metres high in the colour of the season's woods, one for each spur of the hills. (Found by asking the scene what stood there, after two guesses at its material had changed nothing.) The sheet's inner seventy metres are now brought down to the nearer ground's own height (`inner` in `layMountains`), so the hills rise out of the valley's land and the turned-down edge is underground. With it: tree crowns are no longer painted on steep far ground, where they were drawn out into pillars of their own; trees of cards stand closer on the nearest slopes and on steeper ones; and the coarse ground beyond the valley is rock where it is steeper than about forty degrees, where it was smooth turf.

**Mountains.** (For a long time the sheet of the mountains was wound the wrong way and drawn inside out, so that every slope facing the eye was thrown away and only the far sides of ridges showed. If mountains ever look like teeth standing in a pale lake, look at that first.) There are three ranges, one behind another, so that looking out there is always a nearer hill in front of a further one: wooded foothills some three hundred metres off, the mountains behind them at five hundred, and far behind those, at a mile, a range several times as high, which is what goes up into the cloud. Between each two the ground comes down again, and that is where mist lies. All three are one sheet, to three kilometres. The heights are ridged noise summed the way water would have it: each finer layer counts for less where the layers before it are already steep, which leaves sharp crests with long flanks and gullies between. No layer is finer than the sheet can draw where it lies, or it would come out as needles; the sheet is coarser further off, so the far side of a range is plainer. Then anything steeper than rock holds is worn down to what it holds, a point at a time against its lowest neighbour, which takes off the towers two points wide that the noise still leaves and keeps broad faces.

**A hall under the hill.** A hall is far too big for the band of rock that is carved with the land, and too deep: the land's rock goes only a little below the valley floor. So only the doorway and the head of the stair are cut into the land's rock, in the same way a grotto is. From there on the stair and the hall are a body of their own, carved in two overlapping pieces with the carver that keeps edges, from air that has no curve in it: floors, walls and the faces of pillars are planes, and the only shaping is a spreading head and foot to each pillar and a roof that rises to a crown along the middle. Where the two carvings overlap the land's is thrown away. The stair is a slope in the rock with steps of stone laid on it, counted from the bottom so that the first is no higher than the rest: a step and a bit stops the player dead. The doorway at the face is dressed with two jambs, a lintel and a sill, set where the cliff is found to stand a doorway high. The air there is told it is under the hill: a box about the hall has no sky, no sun and no mist in it, only dust.

**Light under the hill.** Nothing out of doors is baked, and rock out there is lit as if the sky stood over it, with never less than three tenths of it, which is right for a crack in a cliff and wrong for a hall: the first hall was an even grey from end to end, and looked sad. A hall now has light of its own, reckoned when it is built, for every point of its rock, and carried in the same numbers the bake fills for the house (for each colour a level and the way the light comes from, so the grain of the stone still catches it). What is reckoned: the last of the day down the stair; every torch, as far as fourteen metres; and in a great hall the sky seen through the mouth of the shaft, cold and from above, and the light the sunlit floor throws back, warm and from below. Each is looked for along a straight line through the hall's own air, the same function it was carved from, so pillars and bridges throw shadows and no torch lights the far side of a wall. Rock that has light of its own is marked as seeing no sky at all, and the material takes that, together with the fixed share of sun only unbaked rock carries, to mean under the hill: the sun's lamp is kept off it, and its own light is used whether or not the house's bake has finished. The four torches nearest the player are real lights besides, as in the house.

**A great hall, and its shaft of day.** The shaft is a round hole cut through the roof, leaning towards the sun (taken as no lower than about fifty degrees, or it would light only its own side), placed so that its light falls where the lower bridge begins; at night it is the moon's. Three things make it. Rock that stands clear in the shaft's light is found when the hall is built, by looking up the shaft from each point, and told so; the material then draws the edge of the light itself for each point of the picture, so the round on the floor is round and not the shape of the carving's cells, and the upper bridge throws its shadow across it. The air in the shaft is lit in the post-processing: how much of each line of sight lies inside a round column is reckoned outright, with a wider, fainter column about it, and the dust is uneven. And the rest of the hall has the two lights that come of it, above. The hall's air is given a little of the sky's colour everywhere, which is what makes its far end blue. Everything else about a great hall is its air: one function of planes, as for any hall, with the ledge, the stair, the screen and its two tiers of openings, the chasm, the two bridges (level on top, arched beneath) and the room over the bridge added to it or taken from it. It is carved in three overlapping pieces, at cells of 42 centimetres; the screen is two cells thick and the bridges two at the crown, which is as thin as that carver can be trusted with. Steps are stones laid on slopes, as on every stair here. The player can fall from the ledge or into the chasm and comes to no harm; the guard that puts a player who has fallen out of the world back where they came in is told how deep the ground goes.

**Stairs and storeys.** A stair is built with the lower of its two rooms: courses of stone a step high, each a step shorter than the one under it, hard against the wall, so it is solid all the way down and is climbed as the steps in a doorway are. The floor above is laid in pieces round the well it comes up through, boards over a room and flags on the ground; the ceiling below is boards on beams with the same well left in it, and a beam that would cross the well stops at its edge. The well begins lower down the flight than a man's height alone would ask, because whoever climbs is a stride deep as well as a man high, and the beams hang below the boards. The thickness of the floor shows round the well and is boarded in; a rail of posts and two bars stands on the two sides that are neither wall nor the head of the stair, because a hole in a floor is walked into. A room with a room over it has no solid above it, and one with a cellar under it none below: those solids are what the light bake and the sun's shadow take for hill, and would fill the other room with dark.

**Lights other than torches.** Decided with the torches, each from a stream of its own, and each burning, lighting its room and baked as a torch is; a room that has one has a torch fewer on its walls. A *hearth* in every kitchen and in some great rooms and chambers (refectory, chapter house, dormitory, solar, gatehall): against a wall with three metres clear of doors, windows, a stair and a vault's pilasters (under a vault, on an end wall), a hearthstone, a cheek of dressed stone either side, a mantel across them, a hood over that and a breast up to the ceiling; the back black with soot, two iron dogs and three logs (each two square rods turned an eighth of a turn to one another, which from a step away is a round log), and a fire of three broad flames along them. Over the hearth of a house that stands free, a chimney stack of ashlar on the wall rises through the roof to above its ridge, with a coping and the dark of the flue. A *ring of candles* hung over the middle of some great rooms where there is height for it: an iron ring with six candles in dishes, on three chains to a hook and one chain from the hook to the ceiling, some three metres up. A *standing candelabrum* of iron in a corner of some chambers, the sanctum among them, clear of doors and stairs and of the middle of each wall: three splayed feet, a shaft with a knop, an arm with a candle at each end and one above in the middle. The candles' flames are the torch's, small, one on each wick, all drawn with the one material.

**What is on the tables and shelves** (`furnish.ts`). Small things, chosen from a stream of their own so that adding them moved no piece of furniture: turned things (a profile of radius and height swept round, smooth at the side, flat at the top and the foot, the top another colour where something is in it) and small squared ones. A refectory's tables have a bowl of turned wood at most places down either side, often a cup of earthenware or stoneware by it, and down the middle a jug, a loaf or a candle in a dish every metre or so; a scriptorium's desks a book lying open, pale leaves in a binding, an inkwell with a quill standing in it and a candle; a kitchen's table an iron pot, bowls, a loaf and a jug. The shelves of a scriptorium, a vestry, a reliquary, an antechamber or a hidden chamber hold books: runs standing against the back, each of a height, a depth and a binding of its own (calf, dyed leather, vellum), close together with a gap now and then and here and there a short pile lying flat; those of a kitchen, a store, a strongroom or an undercroft crocks, jugs and bowls. Not every board is full. Beds have a blanket of wool, undyed or dyed, over the foot, falling a little over the sides, and a bolster at the head (the end nearer a wall); a workshop's benches a mallet, offcuts of board, a pot of glue and an iron blade or two; a cell's crate a bowl and a jug. A forsaken house has about half as much, and its candles are not lit.

**The garth of a cloister.** Inside the cloister walk, in seven cloisters in ten (not under snow), a garden: plots of grass behind low kerbs of stone, four where the garth has room both ways and two, one either side of the well, where it has room only one way; the paths between them cross at the well. The grass is close set and of a deeper green than the meadow's, growing in the shade of the walls; short where the house is kept and long where it is not; here and there a plot has flowers, red, gold, white or purple.

**A tower standing free.** A house in the hill has only inner faces to its walls: nobody ever sees the other side. A tower is seen from both, and its outer face is made exactly as an inner one is, as the inner face of a place imagined on the far side of the wall, which is what an outer face is. So the same courses, the same openings and the same dressed stone round them serve both, and a doorway or a slit is one hole cut through two faces with a sill laid through the thickness between. The faces inside go up storey by storey and each meets the next, because where a stair's well opens the floor the wall behind shows in the thickness of the floor. Stairs are those of the house, turned end for end at each storey; each begins a stride from one wall and ends most of a stride from the other, since a body cannot turn onto a stair that starts in a corner. The ground is levelled under it and a little way out, and nothing is planted within. A tower is scenery: it is not baked. So its stones are told which of them are indoors (everything within the walls and under the roof), and the stone and timber material does with that what the rock material does: no sunlight where there is no sky. Without it the sun shone through the roof onto every floor.

**The shape of the valley.** The site document gives the valley's two sides as lines, and everything about the land is worked out from how far a point is from the nearer of them: more than nothing between them, less outside. That distance is worked out once on a lattice over the valley and read from it. The rock rises from the lines in a stride or two, as it did from the sides of the box, but to a height that changes along them (six metres to about twenty), and behind it the land climbs on as hillside for seventy or ninety metres, more on some sides than on others. Beyond the gap at the foot the same rise is spread over a broader and broader slope until the crags have become hillsides, and the floor runs on between them as an outer vale. At the head, the valley's sides start from the corners of the house; north of the house front everything is side except the house and the hill behind it, so the land either side of the house is the top of the crags run on, and not a scarp of its own.

The band of ground that is carved as rock follows the lines. It is made of rows across the valley, each some ten metres deep; in each row, whatever stretches lie within the band (six metres outside a side, ten inside it, more where a terrace stands out, and past the gap at the foot as much as thirty more outside, where the crag dies away over a slope that broadens behind the side: laid as the plain sheet, the crag went on there from where the band stopped as a wall of smooth grey, the seam a straight line down it) are each carved as one piece; the first row is the head of the valley about the house and is carved whole. How deep a point lies in the band, which says where the rock may be worn and where it must still meet the ground beside it, is measured on the lattice of heights from the nearest point that is not band. The barriers that keep anyone from leaving by the rock are short lengths that follow each side a little behind it, and one crosses the track a few strides beyond the gap.

**A house that stands free.** Its rooms are built as they are under a hill; what changes is what the hill hid. The outline of the house is every room's rectangle grown by a wall's thickness (where the outer face of its walls is), traced on a lattice ten centimetres fine into runs of wall each looking one way, and every run is faced with ashlar from below the ground to the roofline (the height the courts open from), with a plinth along its foot and a cornice along its top. The stretch of the front that meets the forecourt is built with the forecourt, as before, but no broader than the house. The windows of the rooms behind that stretch are cut through it too (they were once left out of it, and so were walled up from outside while open from inside, where the face of a wall cannot be seen from behind), and keep clear of the bay that stands forward round the door. Windows are cut through both faces of an outer wall and the solid between, as the windows between rooms and courts are: tall and arched in a room lit by day, every three metres or so; one narrow slit in a room lit by torches; none in a dark room, so that what is dark stays dark. They keep clear of doors, of the ribs of a vault and of any stair against that wall. Every room that is not a court has a hipped roof of tiles over its walls' middle lines and a little beyond, from the cornice to a ridge along its length, on a plate of stone that lies on the wall tops and fills the hand's breadth between them and the roof, which could otherwise be seen through where no cornice hides it; where two rooms meet, their roofs cross. Nothing of the hill is made: no hill sheet, no turf or heather on top, no rock beside the house, no fence across its front. The ground is level about it, its sheet goes to the walls and no further, and nothing grows inside it.

**What a house that stands free has outside.** Buttresses go where a vault's ribs come down inside (the same places torches stand on pilasters within), and then evenly every four or five metres along any stretch longer than five metres, except within half a metre of a window or of another buttress; each is two stages, a deep lower one and a shallower upper one, each finished with a sloping weathering. Every outer corner has a clasping buttress on each face. A string course runs round the whole outline at three and a half metres. Every roof has a line of ridge tiles. The sanctum's roof is gabled, not hipped, at a steeper pitch, its gables walls of ashlar in courses that step in as the roof narrows, with a bell-cote of two piers, a capping and a bell on the gable nearer the front. The kitchen and the refectory (or a warming house) have a stone chimney through the roof beside the ridge. The entrance bay stands sixty centimetres forward of the front, with the door dressed through its depth, and rises over the cornice in four stepped courses. A cloister walk is built round any court at least seven and a half metres across: slender columns on bases with capitals, a stride and a half in from the walls, none before a doorway; a beam along their heads; and a lean-to roof of tiles from a metre above the beam at the wall down to a hand beyond it. The well stands in the middle of such a court.

**A burnt house.** It is built as any timber house is, through a mesh that chars what it is given: every vertex of timber is darkened to about a quarter, plaster to two fifths and stone to three fifths, and more the higher it stands (soot rises), with a little unevenness from place to place; glass, flowers and tiles are given a mesh that is thrown away. The roof and its gables are not built, nor the ceiling over the top storey, and three charred beams lie fallen at angles on each floor.

**A way through the hill.** The tunnel's air is one function: a rough tube a stride and a half wide and a little over a man's height, bending a little, its floor going evenly from the chamber's to the arm's and coming up a hand or two out in the arm so that it meets the foot of the rock as a ramp. Three carvings take it in, as a hall's stair is taken in by two. The chamber's carving has it among its clefts, so the chamber's wall opens. The land's carving has it with the land's own air, so the mouth opens in the ridge's crag; but none of the land's rock is kept from thirty centimetres inside the ridge's line inward, near the tunnel. From there to the chamber a carving of its own makes it, in the chamber's group, so it is baked with the house. The rock in the mouth darkens going in. The fence that runs up the ridge's side has a gap over it, and nothing grows or lies before the mouth.

**The cavern under the arm.** The way down is a tube along a line of three legs (along the ridge, out under its side, and in to the cavern's near rim), broadest at knee height and drawing in to the roof, falling evenly and level for its last three metres so that it meets the cavern's floor and not a ledge above it. The cavern is an uneven dome over a rough oval floor, lowest at its edge; stalagmites stand on the floor with a shorter stalactite over each, none within two and a half metres of where the passage comes in. Both are carved with the tunnel, in the chamber's group. The land's carving keeps no rock near the passage below the level it starts from. Three things that are not rock had to know of it: the barrier up the ridge's side has a second gap, where the passage goes under it; the barrier along the valley's side stops above the cavern's roof where the cavern reaches beneath it; and the scene records its lowest floor (`lowest`), so that the dark plane under everything lies below the cavern and a body standing in it is not taken to have fallen out of the world.

**The passage from the cave in the arm.** Its line is the document's (`joinLine`): out of the side of the cave's chamber, along the foot of the arm's wall 3.4 metres behind it, and into the nearest part of the cavern's rim. It has the same section as the other way down, leaves the cave level for two metres, falls evenly, and is level for its last three. Three carvings make it. The land's has it as far as a seam 1.6 metres outside the chamber's wall, so that the wall opens (the land's lattice is taken deeper there to reach the passage's floor). The cavern's has its last three metres, so that the cavern's wall opens. Between the two it has a carving of its own, on the cavern's lattice so that those two meet exactly; at the other end it begins 70 centimetres before the land's leaves off, because the two lattices differ and where they only met there were chinks one could see the hillside through. Where the cavern lies beyond the arm, all of the fall of the way from the house is inside the ridge, and it crosses under the arm level. No stalagmite stands within a metre of the straight way from either entrance to the cavern's middle. Slabs that are drawn only into the sun's shadow lie over the cavern and along the passage: behind the valley's side nothing else above them casts a shadow, and without them sunlight showed on their walls in jagged patches. The barrier along the valley's side stops above the passage, and above the way from the house, wherever either runs behind the side or crosses under it.

**From a hall.** The door onward is part of the hall's air (`hallOnward` says where): a doorway 2.2 metres wide and 2.6 high, squared, through the side wall of a plain hall or the far wall of the room over a great hall's upper bridge. The passage begins inside it, 60 centimetres short of its end, and where the two overlap the passage's air is the door's as well, so that the hall's carving and the passage's make the same thing. The hall carves as far as ten centimetres short of the door's end and the passage's own carving begins half a metre before that. (At both seams, cave and hall, the passage's own carving is made seven centimetres narrower where it overlaps the other, widening to its true size within eighty centimetres: two carvings of the same thing on different lattices came through each other in patches.)

**What is beyond the edge of the light.** The sun's shadow and the open sky are each reckoned from a picture taken over the box of the house and the valley (the scene's `bounds`). Rock under the hill outside that box cannot be shadowed, whatever is put over it, and air outside it is taken to be open. Three things follow. Rock of the cavern and its ways that lies outside the box (`offMap`) is carved, on the same lattice, as a second mesh of the kind a hall is made of, which is told it sees no sky. The cavern and the passage to it have a box of their own (`underDeep`, beside the halls' `unders`) inside which there is no sky and no sun in the air; from a cave it stops below the arm's ground, so that no open air is in it. And mist is given a floor (`groundLow`, the lowest ground of the valley, less half a metre, fading out over a metre and a half below that): it lay thickest in whatever was dug below the valley.

**The charcoal burners' camp** (`camp.ts`). Nearly all of it is turned work: a profile, how far out at each height, swept round an upright line, which is what a mound of earth, a hut of poles, a barrel, a stump and a sack all are. The side that shows is the one on the left hand going along the profile, so a hump lying on the ground is given from its outer edge over the top to its inner. Earth and turf are the ground's own material, told how far each point is bare, with the ground's own colour at that place, so that a clamp is of the same earth it stands on; the hut's turf is laid in nine courses, each standing out a little over the one below. Low things (the kerb, the raked-out ring, ash) follow the ground; upright things stand level and have a skirt going down into it. The ground itself is levelled under the camp, as it is under a ring of stones, and the floor of the clearing counts as within doors for everything that plants or strews. Smoke is three upright sheets crossed through one line, narrow at the foot and spreading, grey whatever the light, drawn with the water because the spray of a fall is made the same way. The burners' fire is a torch with no bracket and a larger flame, and takes a real light when the player is near, as torches do.

**The mine.** Its air is one function, measured as a hall's is (out of the valley, to one side, up): the adit with a roof cut to a slight arch, the crosscut, and the working with steps left at the back. The land's carving has only the mouth; from there three carvings that overlap a little make the adit, the working and the crosscut, in the kind of rock that sees no sky. Timber underground is of that kind too; the portal, the first sets and everything in the yard are ordinary. The spoil heap is not a thing set on the ground but ground (`heapAt`), so that it can be walked up, the turf's material shows it bare, and nothing is planted on it; the yard counts as within doors for whatever plants or strews. The tramway out of doors lies on whichever is lower, the ground or the floor of the cutting before the mouth. Wheels are turned work lying on its side (`turned.ts`, shared with the camp).

**Dripstone.** In the cavern each thing that has grown is a radius that changes with height, about a line that leans a little: a stalagmite tapers as the power 1.2 of what is left of its height, swells and draws in along it, has three ribs round it, and spreads at the foot; a column is thinnest at its middle; a stalactite is the same hung from the roof, and where the roof is too low to leave headroom under one it dwindles to nothing (it must not simply stop: a thing that is there at one point and gone at the next is carved as a shard). They are no thinner than about thirty centimetres at the root, since the carving's cells are twenty-eight. From a hall the passage is of the hall's kind of rock, which is told it sees no sky; without that the sun showed on its walls just outside the door.

**A delve.** A delve is laid out as a house under the hill, and what makes it a delve is which rooms are caves (see `makeDelve` in the generator). In the mesher it differs only at its front: there is no house front across the forecourt, but the escarpment across all of it, carved from the land as the rock beside a house front is, with a recess cut square into it at the door, a little broader and higher than the portal and running back through the rock to the entrance's wall. In the recess stands the portal: a face of ashlar with the door in it, dressed jambs and a moulded head. The meadow's piece of land is carved without the trick that lays the ground before the door up to the house front, so the rock comes down to the forecourt. There is no ivy, and no coping on top.

**A village and its keep.** The houses of a village are the timber houses of a hamlet, and are built as they are. The keep stands on a promontory, which is a terrace like any other but high and broad and marked as the keep's: its front is a cliff of carved rock, and its ledge climbs along the side wall. The gentle swells of the ground are faded out over its top, so that a castle's court is level, and over some metres, so the ledge meets it without a step; the ledge and the shelf are left as they were made when the rock is worn. A castle's walls are built by what builds the castles on the far hills, at three fifths of that size, bare inside and solid, with its gate turned towards the end the ledge comes up at; within stands the keep's tower, built as the valley's towers are but raised to the top of the promontory.

**The ridge and the arms.** The house front is not the head of the valley but the end of a ridge, recorded in the document with the valley's sides: it is as broad as the head of the valley or as the house with eight metres either side, whichever is broader, and runs north without end. Within it, the ground is the house's own hill, as it always was over the house, except along its edges, which are crag like the valley's sides. North of the house front each side of the valley is an arm's outer wall, which comes back to the ridge at the arm's head; where a side has no arm it lies along the ridge, and there is nothing between. The arm's floor rises four or five metres as it goes north. The scarp either side of the house front, the solid rock beside the house, and the fence along the house front all stop at the ridge's edge, so as not to stand across an arm's mouth; a fence runs up each side of the ridge that has an arm beside it.

**Footpaths.** A footpath is a line of points in the document. Its distance is worked out on a lattice over the valley, as the sides' is, and written into the ground's field as two hundred plus that distance (the track writes a hundred plus its own), so that the material for ground can tell them apart: a footpath is one worn line a forearm's breadth or two across, wandering a little, with dead grass along its edges, where the track is two ruts. Grass is short along it, and trees, boulders, undergrowth and fallen wood keep off it.

**Buildings that stand free.** A house in the hill is masonry laid stone by stone, because its walls are seen from a pace away and by torchlight. A building in the valley is seen first from across it, and is made another way: of plain solids, each one thing of the building. A wall with openings in it is what is left of the wall (the piers between, the breast under each opening and the head over it), each a block; a window is a frame, its bars and a dark pane behind them, set back in the wall's thickness; a timber is a squared length from one point to another, standing a little proud of the plaster between timbers (each layer of a frame at its own depth, a centimetre and more from the next: posts and beams in front, rails and studs behind them, braces behind those, the two braces of a cross apart, the plaster at the back, so that no two faces lie in one plane and flicker); a moulding is a block that stands out further than the one under it; a roof is two sheets with thickness, tiled above and boarded beneath, lying over gables that are framed like the walls. Nothing is drawn on, so the sun finds every edge and every piece throws its shadow, which is most of what makes a front read as architecture. A building is laid out in measures of its own, along each wall from its left end as seen from outside, up from its ground, and into the wall from its face, so one description of a wall serves all four sides and either way the building faces. The two styles differ only in what they put on a wall. The timber-framed house frames each jettied storey bay by bay: where a bay has a window, a head rail, two jambs, and under the sill a cross more often than not; where it has none, a brace leaning the way of its neighbours. The classical building cuts its ground storey into courses with the joints struck deep, hangs a surround, a sill on blocks and a cornice on brackets about each tall window, and runs its cornice round as three mouldings each standing further out than the last. Plaster is one pale texture and takes its wash from the colour given to each block; clay tile is a texture of round-ended tiles in lapped rows; sheet metal and glass are the plaster's surface made smooth, the glass dark, so that windows mirror the sky. The tower is still masonry, and borrows the plain solids for its plinth, bands, corbels and porch.

**Variations of the classical building.** Besides its breadth, its storeys, its wings and whether its middle stands forward, a classical building may differ in these ways, dealt from streams of their own (`classicalVariations` in `core/building.ts`), so that adding them changed nothing else about a place. Its storeys are not always a tall principal storey over a lower ground one: in about two in seven the ground storey is the tall one, four and a half to five metres, as a counting house's or a row of shops' is, with lower storeys alike over it; and in about two in seven all its storeys are about the same, the ground one a little taller. Windows are as tall as their storeys allow, so the fronts differ with them. A broad one, eleven bays or more, may have two or three entrances along its front, as a long block of lodgings has: two set out either side of the middle, or the middle one and one either side, each with its door and steps, its hall and its stair, the rooms between opening towards the nearest of them (`planEntrances`); where the middle stands forward and has no door, rooms lie behind it. About three in ten whose middle stands forward have no pediment over it: the four pilasters go up to the cornice, which runs straight across. And about two in three with wings, an L or a U, have towers at one to three of their corners, rising one to three storeys over the cornice: at both ends of the front, at the far ends of a U's wings, or one alone, and a third at a wing's end. A tower is a square of the front's own colour five to six and a half metres across, its walls a hand proud of the front so that the low wall over the cornice is lost in them, a window or two in every face of every storey dressed as the upper storeys' are, a band at each floor and strips at its corners, a cornice of its own, and a pyramid roof of the same seamed metal at thirty-six degrees with an iron finial and a gilt knob. The roofs below run on into it. Nothing in a tower can be reached. And a classical building without wings, with two bays free at an end of the middle that holds the door and the stair, may have a carriage way through that end of its ground storey, from the street to the yard behind: a round arch in the front and in the back, three metres or so broad and rising nearly to the ground storey's ceiling, between the two end bays' windows (which are not there on the ground storey), its head filled through the wall's whole thickness, a moulded archivolt about it with a keystone and imposts, and in the front's arch two gates of boards standing open against the sides of the way. The way is at the street's level, walled in the front's wash over a stone dado and paved in setts; the rooms over it are the end rooms of every other storey. In a town every closed block of classical buildings has at least one (`docs/05-site-format.md`, `arch`). `lab/buildings.html` shows them.

**How a classical building is dressed.** (19 November 2026, from a picture of the Government Palace on Helsinki's Senate Square.) A classical front may be anything from a plain block of three bays with nothing on it but its windows, its bands and its cornice to a palace of twenty-five, and what lies between is its `dress` (document 5): how broad its middle is and whether columns stand before it on a ground storey brought forward, pavilions at its ends, round heads, the heads over its windows, wreaths, a balustrade, a clock in the pediment, a flagpole, what its roof is covered with, how high its floor stands and how its ground falls, and the style of its walls. The styles are four, and each says what is put on a wall: *empire*, strips at the corners from the band over the ground storey to the cornice, surrounds with sills on blocks and the heads the dress names, three bands, a cornice of four mouldings on blocks; *quoins*, as the Renaissance did, stones laid long and short up the corners in place of the strips, a little greyer than the dressings, and the surrounds eared; *lesenes*, as Nordic classicism did, flat strips the whole height of the corners, plain frames, one band and a cornice of three mouldings without blocks, and the order of the middle flattened to strips; *bare*, the openings with their sills, one band and a slim cornice. A building stands level whatever its ground does: where the ground falls along its front, its granite base shows the more towards the lower end, a basement's barred windows appear in it where the floor is a metre or more up, no window is cut where the ground stands against it, and every flight of steps reaches down to the ground before it, with granite parapets where it climbs high. Where two parts of a building meet at a corner, one runs past the other's face and the other stops short: a corner of rusticated work is mitred course by course; anything laid level with or over another, a sill on the wall under its window, a band on a band, is a centimetre off it. And no window looks into a wall: one that would face another building nearer than about two and a half metres, under its eaves, is not made (`near`, `blinded`).

**Inside a classical building.** The plan is worked out when the site is generated and kept in the site document; the builder and the floor plans both read it, and the measures they share (where windows stand, how thick walls are, where the stair goes) are written once, in `core/building.ts`. The plan comes from the front. Each window is a bay; a wall between rooms may stand only midway between two windows; so the bays are dealt out: one in the middle, or three if the middle stands forward or the house is seven bays or more, for the entrance and the stair; the rest to the two wings, in rooms of one, two or three bays, the same on both sides. A wall down the length of the house divides front rooms from back, and the end walls are given an even number of windows so that it meets them between two. Walls are thick, as they were: an outer wall is three quarters of a metre, of which the outer thirty centimetres are the rendered skin with its windows set in it, and the rest belongs to the rooms; the wall down the middle is half a metre, the walls between rooms thirty centimetres. A room is its clear floor, and each of its walls is a lining that stands out from that floor into the wall: half of a wall it shares with a neighbour, all of an outer wall inside the skin. Two linings back to back are a wall, and the same holes cut in both are a doorway with a deep reveal; at a window the reveal inside is more than half a metre deep, with a board for a sill. Each lining runs on past the room's corners by the depth of the linings it meets, so that no corner is left open. Doors are placed by rule (a stride in from the windows for the line of doors along the front, the middle of the room for the door to the room behind). The stair is two flights side by side with a landing between, of steps a hand and a half high or less, however high the storey: so a taller storey has more steps and a longer flight, and each flight begins at the same place so that the one above stands over the one below. Floors are laid room by room, and where a stair comes up, round its well. Indoors is told it sees a third of the sky, which dims it, and glass throws no shadow, so the sun comes in.

**Inside a timber house.** Planned in the site document storey by storey, since each storey stands further out over the street than the one below and the walls between rooms stand at posts of the frame, which are in different places on each. The rules of the frame that both the plan and the builder need (how many bays a wall has, which of them have windows) are in `core/building.ts`, with the stair. A wall between rooms is built as the outer walls are: a plate, posts at a stride, a rail at the height of a hand, the panels between washed white, and each doorway framed. Floors are a sheet of boards with the stair's well cut out of it, on joists at the same spacing and in the same places as the joist ends that show under the jetty outside, cut short at the well with a trimmer at each end. The stair's flights are all the same length on plan, so each begins over the foot of the one below and all come up through the same well; the steps are steeper than a classical stair's (up to 22 cm, on treads of 21 to 29), which the body takes by stepping up. The lowest flight is solid to the floor. The flights above are treads only: a flight with risers and a soffit hung too low over the heads of those coming up the flight beneath it wherever the storey was a low one. Along the open side of every flight is a screen of slats from the floor to the handrail; on a landing that same screen is what stands between the floor and the well, and on the top floor, where nothing goes up, a level rail does it, along the well and across its end. The door stands open, back against the hall's wall.

**Wings.** A wing is three more walls, laid out as the main block's are and finished with the same cornice and attic, and its roof is hipped at the far end and runs in at the near end under the main block's, which is higher; where the two meet they simply cross, as roofs do. The main block's back wall stays where it was, thick as ever, with no windows where a wing stands against it and a door through it into the wing on every storey. That door is the one case of an opening cut through a skin between two rooms, so openings are recorded by where they lie across the thickness of the wall, and a lining takes those whose skin it reaches, from either side. The rooms of a wing are a single row, joined by doors near the middle of the wing's width. The ground is left out under the whole outline, and its corners drawn to the nearest point of that outline that is not inside another part of the building.

**A building on a slope.** The site document says how much higher the ground stands at the back of such a building than before its door. The ground about it is shaped to that: level before the door for the depth of its steps, rising along its sides from front to back, and behind it standing at the height of the ground floor and running on back towards the valley's side for twenty metres and more before it gives way to the land, so that it reads as a hillside the building was set into and not as a mound heaped against it. The sheet of the ground is left out under the building and its corners drawn to the building's walls, or it would come up through the floors. Any window whose sill would be under the ground is left out of the wall, outside and in. A door in the back wall opens onto the ground there, at the height of the ground floor, and the bands that run along the wall are broken for it.

**Terraces and grottoes.** A terrace is put into the plain shape of the ground before anything is carved: a shelf against the wall with all its depth where it is level and a path's width on the way up, so the wearing and the carving treat it as they treat the rest, and its front comes out a cliff like any other. More rock is carved where it stands out past the band of cliff. Along the way up the rock keeps almost none of its roughness, or the rock's own forms would eat the path. Where the way is steps, the steps are blocks laid on the slope for the eye; what is walked on is the slope, so no foot catches on a riser. A grotto cannot be put into the heights at all, since heights cannot say "hollow". It is given as air of its own, a passage and a chamber, and wherever land is carved the two are taken together: what is air in either is air. Its floor is set from the lowest ground before its mouth as the wearing left it, and ramps up outside, so there is never a sill. Rock out of doors is lit as if the whole sky stood over it, which inside a grotto it does not; the rock there is simply made darker the further in it is. The fence that keeps the player in the valley begins, over a grotto, above its roof.

**On the hills.** Landmarks on the far hills are placed before the sheet of the far land is laid: the height of the land is a function of its own, so the highest ground on a bearing in a range can be found first, and then the sheet is laid with a level top there, coming down to the land about it over twice its own breadth, and no tree card standing on it. What stands there is built of large many-sided and square solids, turned to face the valley, with what reads at a distance and nothing else: battlements, pointed roofs, the dark of windows, broken tops. The haze does the rest.

**Far land.** The cliff's material stretched over a hill half a kilometre away showed the same square of stone laid side by side to the horizon, and no sign of anything growing. Far land has a material of its own, and everything that makes it look like country is worked out for each point of the picture as it is drawn. The sheet carries only the colour of the bare stone and, at each of its points, how high that point stands in its range and how much of a hollow it is. From those, the slope and a little noise, the material decides what covers the ground: woods low down and up the gullies, pasture above, bare rock in beds where it is steep, scree under the faces, snow on what is high and not too steep to hold it; each with a ragged edge of its own and not smeared from one corner of the sheet to the next. In the woods it draws the crowns of the trees, each a small dome lit on its sunward side and dark between it and the next, firs smaller and darker than broadleaves and standing in their own stands; and a wood thins out a tree at a time, each tree standing or not, instead of fading. Gullies are light and shade only: unevenness drawn out along the line of fall, since water runs straight down a slope and what it has cut runs that way too; the woods follow them up the hill. All of it fades out as it gets too small to draw. On the two nearer ranges, out to 440 metres, there are also trees: eight or nine thousand single tufts of cards, each with a whole fir or a whole broadleaved tree painted on it, standing where the material paints woods, so that the skyline of a wooded hill is toothed and not smooth. The colours of woods and pasture follow the season. Far land stops nobody and is left out of collision. The rock material itself is now read more and more blurred with distance, so that a far cliff has the stone's colour and none of its pattern.

## Materials

**Working on them.** Rock, ground and leaves are worked on in the material lab (`lab/materials.html`, `npm run lab`; see the README): a small stage with one of everything standing in the open, built in a second by the game's own code and drawn with these same materials, where saving a material's source puts the new one on at once and a kept picture can be wiped against the live one. Look there first, close, at walking distance and far, in more than one light and on more than one stone; then look in a valley.

Every surface uses a physically based material: base colour, normal, roughness, ambient occlusion and height, with metalness and emission where relevant.

**Leaves, seen close.** (17 November 2026, in the lab.) A spray of leaves was painted in flat greys, two tones to a leaf, on a flat card, and lit as part of the crown's rounded body only: close to, a paper cut-out. Three things changed in `vegetation.ts`. *The paint*: at 1024 points, each leaf darker at its foot and lighter at its tip, with a pale midrib and side veins and a cast of its own, yellower or bluer; twigs brown and thinner. *How each leaf lies*: a second picture, painted with the first and carried on it (`texture.userData.relief`), says which way every point of every leaf faces: each leaf tipped its own way, folded into a shallow trough along its midrib, drooping from foot to tip. The material lights a leaf by that and by the crown's round body together, six parts to six, so the crown keeps its light and dark sides and within it one leaf gleams and its neighbour does not; leaves are also less rough, and throw back more of the sun. *The card*: creased down its middle (four triangles for two), so its halves face different ways. Leaf triangles are doubled by this; no frame has been timed since. Bark was also made matt: with the ordinary sheen a round trunk mirrored the sky down both sides and stood pale blue. *Sprigs.* All of that still left a crown made of sheets, each with sixty leaves painted on it, and a sheet seen edge on is a streak. **Bark.** (17 November 2026, against a picture of an old trunk in plates and flakes.) Three things. **Grass with distance.** Grass is drawn three ways. Within 15 metres, real blades. From there to the grass range (80 metres at the highest level of detail), painted tufts; the two give way to each other tuft by tuft over the outer half of the blade range, and were brought to one brightness, since blades came out lighter than tufts and the difference showed as an edge however gradual the change. **Where textures come from.** The primary source is a procedural material library: recipes that generate tiling texture sets on the GPU from noise and pattern functions, with normal maps derived from the generated height. This suits the project. Materials are seedable and can vary per site and per culture, nothing has to be licensed or downloaded, and the generator can ask for "this masonry, but older and wetter". The pipeline also accepts ordinary scanned texture sets, so openly licensed libraries can be dropped in where a recipe is not good enough.

**Putting them on generated geometry.**

- World-aligned and triplanar projection, so nothing needs hand-made texture coordinates and courses of masonry run true around corners.
- Layered blending by field: a base material with moss where it is damp and lit, dark staining where water runs, soot where there is fire, lichen where it is exposed, dust where nothing walks, polish where everything does. Blends follow the height map so moss sits in the joints before it covers the stone.
- Wetness as a modifier: darker and smoother near water and after rain.
- Anti-repetition: stochastic tiling and large-scale colour variation so no pattern is visible across a big wall or hillside.
- Depth: parallax on strongly relieved surfaces such as cobbles and rough masonry.

**Ground.** Everything nearly level out of doors has one material, and what it draws is decided for each point of the picture, not for each point of the sheet. **Depth, and how much trouble is taken at what distance.** Real displacement, moving the surface itself, wants a mesh several times finer than any here and cannot be had cheaply in a browser. ## Lighting

### Light regimes

| Setting | Key light | Fill | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open air | Sun or moon with shadows across the whole view | Sky, by time of day and weather | Long shadows, aerial haze, changing colour through the day |
| Interior | Daylight through openings; fires, candles and lanterns | Light bounced from floors and walls | Pools and shafts; warm against cool |
| Underground | Placed sources and whatever the player carries | Almost none, which is the point | Darkness as the default; every light is a decision |

### Approach

Once generated, the world is static, and that is the opportunity. Soft indirect light — sun bouncing off a courtyard floor into an arcade, torchlight warming a vault — is what makes a scene look real, and it is too costly to compute every frame in a browser. It can be computed once.

- **Baked indirect light and occlusion**, calculated after generation by tracing rays against the site's geometry, progressively and in the background so the player can start walking while it refines.
- **Real-time direct light** for the sun and for the nearest few sources, with shadows, so flames flicker and the carried light moves.
- **A probe volume** carrying the baked light to anything that moves.
- **Many small lights** handled by baking the distant ones and keeping only the nearby ones live.

**How the bake is stored.** Per vertex. Because every stone is modelled, vertices are never more than a few tens of centimetres apart, which is fine enough for soft indirect light and avoids unwrapping generated geometry for lightmaps. Crevices such as mortar joints get their own vertices, so they darken naturally.

Each vertex stores, per colour channel, a constant term and a direction term, so that the light a surface receives still depends on which way the normal map tilts it. For a vertex whose hemisphere is sampled uniformly with N rays of incoming radiance L along directions d:

- constant term E0 = (π / 2) · mean(L)
- direction term E1 = π · mean(L · d)
- light received by a surface with normal n: E0 + E1 · n

The bake also records how much sky each vertex sees and what share of the day it is in direct sun. Moss grows where there is sky but little sun, and sky reflections are suppressed where the sky cannot be seen.

A final pass averages nearby vertices that face the same way to remove sampling noise.

**Not every vertex is traced.** A stone has about forty vertices and only a handful are on its face; the rest shape its bevel, its mortar edge and its backing sheet. Light is traced for the face vertices only. The others copy from the nearest face vertex of the same stone, darkened towards the joint, which is where the joint's shadow comes from. This made the bake about seven times faster (230 seconds to 33 on the test scene, same settings) with no visible loss.

### The roof of the house

The house is built into the hill, and its roof is the hill: turf lying a hand's breadth above the top of the walls, swelling and dipping over the vaults, level only where it meets the front wall and the edge of anything open to the sky. Thin soil over stone grows no trees, so it carries rough grass, heather in mounds and a bush here and there. The front wall is finished with a coping, and every court, roofless room and shaft has a kerb of stone round its mouth. The sheet of turf is drawn back to the line of the wall top and out of every opening, so that it ends exactly where masonry begins.

For a long time this turf lay fifteen centimetres *below* the top of the solid volumes that cast the building's shadow, and so was in shadow always: from above, the house was a black shape. Nobody saw it until there was a way to fly. Whatever is laid over the house must lie above those volumes.

### Skin and solid

Visible geometry is only a skin: the faces a player can see, with nothing behind them. Light needs to know where the solid is, for two reasons found in practice.

- **Buried vertices.** Patches overlap at inside corners, so some vertices sit inside a wall or under a floor. Left alone they collect light from wherever their rays escape to, often the open sky, and it smears onto the visible surface next to them as a bright rim. The bake is given the solid volumes behind the skin; a vertex whose rays mostly hit solid mass or the backs of surfaces is treated as buried and takes its light from visible neighbours.
- **Thin shadows.** A skin shadows itself unreliably, so sunlight would catch stone edges inside a closed room. The same solid volumes are drawn into the sun's shadow map from their sunward side, which gives walls and roofs real thickness.

In the hand-assembled scene the solids are listed alongside the skin. In generated sites they come for free: the span grid already says which space is open and which is not.

**Solid things are closed things.** Any free-standing or projecting piece — a coping, a sill, a merlon, a step, a beam, a chest — is built with all six of its faces, including ones that seem hidden against a wall or floor. Leaving out faces "the player cannot see" was tried and was wrong twice: undersides of overhanging stones showed the sky through them. The few extra hidden faces cost little; a see-through stone costs the whole illusion.

Walls are cut exactly at openings. Vertices of a wall that fall inside an opening are moved to its edge, so the stones simply end there; a triangle is left out only if all its corners were inside, or if its corners were moved to opposite sides and it would stretch across. This matters most for a secret door, where no band of dressed stone hides the cut.

Two smaller measures keep the skin closed: every stone has a plain backing sheet just behind its mortar, slightly larger than the stone, so no pixel-wide gaps show between neighbours; and where a wall is cut for an opening, triangles touching the hole are left out and the dressed stones round the opening cover the edge.

### Hours and weather

There are six hours. Morning, midday and evening have the sun well up. At **sunset** it stands a hand's breadth above the hills: long shadows, red light, a sky from gold to deep blue. At **dusk** it has gone: what light there is comes weakly from where it went down, the sky keeps a faint light of its own with a band of rose at the horizon, and the first stars are out. At **night** the strongest light is the moon, cold and faint but casting shadows, under a sky of stars. Each hour gives the real-time light, the bake and the fog their sun and sky, and says how far the eye may open in the dark, which is what keeps night dark without making it black. After sunset the sky is drawn for where the sun really is, below the horizon, while the light comes from the afterglow or the moon. From sunset on, a share of the windows of the houses that stand free and of the fishermen's cottages are lit from within: the glass glows with the room's light, yellow lamplight or redder firelight, every room its own and wavering a little where it is a fire, the windows of one room alike (some two rooms in five in the houses, two cottages in three, told from where each room is, so no choice is drawn). The lamps are no brighter by night than at dusk, since the eyes have opened wider; by day those windows are glass like the rest. About each lit window hangs a soft haze of its own light, as the eye sees any bright thing in the dark: a round some two or three times the window's size, standing half a metre out from it towards whoever looks, so that the wall hides what is behind it and from behind the house the house hides it all (`glow.ts`; the lit panes are found in the geometry already built).

Clouds have volume. They are a layer of air, heaped cloud with its base some eight hundred metres up (from 950 on a nearly clear day down to 600 on a heavy one, lower in rain) and a kilometre or two deep, layered cloud a low thin layer a few hundred metres up, whose thickness at any point is read from a block of noise that repeats in every direction and drifts with the wind: billows with rounded cells cut into them for the shape, finer cells to knot the outline and fray the edges. A piece of air is cloud if the noise there clears a bar; the bar is lower the more cloud the day has, rises with height so that a cloud is broad at its flat base and only its strongest parts tower, and was set by measuring the noise, so that the share of sky asked for is the share covered. Looking up, each view ray is marched through the layer, and at every step a shorter march towards the sun finds how much cloud the light came through, so clouds have lit sides, dark bases and bright edges against the sun, and take the colour of the hour. Looking at the land, the same question is asked of the line from each point to the sun: that is the shadow of the cloud, which drifts over the valley as the cloud does. It dims only what the sun would otherwise reach under open sky. Each site has its own amount of cloud, from nearly clear to heavy.

**Heaped cloud stands high** (19 November 2026, asked for cloudy skies more dramatic and impressive). Heaped cloud was a layer 360 metres deep two or three hundred metres up whatever the day: small soft heaps hanging low over the roofs like smoke, white all over, never the towers of a summer's afternoon. **Edges of what is cut square** (19 November 2026, asked how sharp-cut buildings and stairs could look more real). Nothing cut by hand has an edge as sharp as a box's: a dressed stone's arris is eased by the mason and then by weather, a step's nose by feet, a beam's by the adze and the years, and the rounded edge catches a thin line of light where the two faces turn away from each other, and is chipped and worn paler. The plain solids of the buildings that stand free, the steps, kerbs, cairns and jetties built of anyAngle's solids, and every squared timber had edges sharp as card. Modelling the rounding would be a dozen more triangles to every edge, of which there are hundreds of thousands, so it is drawn in the shading instead (`edgeOf` in `siteMaterial.ts`): every face of such a solid marks its corners (`markFace` in `meshBuilder.ts`, four bytes to a point, `aEdge`), the material works out from how those marks change across the picture how far each point is from each edge of its face in metres and which way the edge lies, whatever the face's shape or size, and within a set width of an edge turns the surface towards it, a little at first and half way to the next face at the edge itself, and makes it a little paler. The width is two centimetres for dressed stone, one and a fifth for timber, three for split rock laid by hand; it fades out where it would be much narrower than a point of the picture. Only stone, timber and rock: plaster and brick walls are built of pieces that abut (piers, breasts and heads between windows), and rounding their edges would draw lines across a wall that is one. The masonry of the house has real bevels already and marks nothing. It is plainest in the sun and close to; in shade it is slight. Where an edge's outline is seen as well as its light, the edge is taken off in the geometry too (`chamfered` in `geom/chamfer.ts`): every face of the solid drawn in by the bevel, a narrow strip along each edge whose points keep the ways their two faces look so that the light turns over it as over a rounded arris, and a small face at each corner; 44 triangles where the square solid has 12. That is done only for what stands out to be looked at: a mountain top's treads (three centimetres), a town's quay coping and the steps down its face, the sills and the cornices over windows, the main cornice's runs, the steps before a door, the treads of stairs inside, and the rails of balustrades (one and a half to two centimetres). A town's triangles went from 5.68 million to 5.80. `?edges=0` draws all of it sharp, as before. (`tileGeometry` now keeps each attribute's own kind of array: it had made every one into floats, which would have read the marks as 0 to 255.)

That holds for every rounded edge, and it was likely behind faint pale lines along other undersides too.

**Tone.** How the light of the scene is brought down to what a screen shows is the film-like ACES curve, as before; `?tone=agx` and `?tone=neutral` show the same place with two others, to compare by eye. AgX lets bright colour pale towards white instead of running to yellow and is greyer and softer in the shade; the neutral curve keeps colours as they are and gives deeper shadows and a bluer sky. Not changed: the choice is one of taste, and the green of the grass and leaves under the present curve is liked.

### Things that move

Doors swing, so they cannot be baked with the walls. Each door leaf is built like the masonry (boards with joints, closed on every side) and takes its share of baked light from the building just in front of each face. Doors do not cast shadows, because all shadow maps are drawn once.

Small loose things — iron fittings, torch brands, keys — get a faint fixed ambient light and the real lights, and nothing else. Without that they are lit by the open sky wherever they are, indoors included, and glow white.

### Darkness and the carried light

Not every place is lit, and that is decided, not left to chance. The site document records how each place is lit: by day (open to the sky, or reached by a court's light through windows and arches), by torches, or not at all. The generator leaves some rooms dark on purpose — side chambers more often than passages, and never two in a row, so there is always light ahead to walk towards.

The player carries a light for those rooms: small, warm, held low and to one side so that it models surfaces instead of flattening them, and weak enough that torchlit rooms still read as torchlit. It can be lowered. It casts no shadows yet, because that would mean redrawing a shadow map of the whole site every frame.

### Light as a design tool

Lights are placed by the generator for reasons (see the lighting layer in document 2): to pull the player forward, to leak information at a fork, to mark what matters, to make a sanctuary feel safe. Darkness is used as deliberately as light. A scene lit evenly everywhere is a failed scene.

## Performance

The target is a steady 60 frames per second in a desktop browser on a mid-range laptop, with quality tiers above and below.

**How it is drawn.** With three.js's WebGPU renderer, which draws with WebGPU where a browser has it and with WebGL 2 where it has not. Nothing in the program is written for either: every material is a standard material whose colour, roughness and normal are small programs of nodes built in TypeScript, and whose lighting is the standard lighting with a few things changed by a lighting model of our own (indirect light from the bake and none from the sky's picture; the sun kept off what is under the hill; reflections of the sky only where sky is seen; light coming through leaves). The air, the clouds, water and flames are programs of nodes too. Three things follow from WebGPU's rules. A mesh may carry eight runs of numbers and no more, so the fourteen numbers of baked light at each point lie side by side in one run. Nothing can be read back from the graphics card in the same breath it was drawn, so the meter that sets the eye's adjustment answers a frame or two late. And a picture with several samples to a point cannot have its depths read as an ordinary picture can, so the water's mirror is drawn with one. Measured on a desktop with a Radeon RX 6800 at 1252 by 737, drawing frames back to back: about 10 milliseconds with a lake in view, of which the mirror's second drawing of the scene is the larger part, and about 13 looking down the track. The WebGL renderer it replaced took 20 and 15 at 1298 by 849.

**What is left out of a frame, and what is not** (November 2026). Grass, flowers and leaves are drawn as instances in blocks of the ground, 24 metres for grass to 220 for the far woods' painted trees, and each block is left out where it is out of view or beyond its kind's range: real blades near the eye, painted tufts beyond, every fourth tuft further still, sprigs of leaves near and painted sprays beyond (`cullCards`). What lies beyond the site (a town's buildings, the land, rock, the far country) was one mesh to a material, whose bounds took in the whole place, so none of it was ever outside the view: in a town all six to eight million of its triangles went to the graphics card every frame, wherever one looked, and were thrown away only after their corners had been worked out. It is now cut into tiles of the ground sixty-four metres square (larger for the far country), so that what is behind the eye or off to the side is not drawn, and what is drawn is drawn nearest first, which leaves less to be painted over (`tileGeometry`; `?tiles=0` for one mesh to a material as before). Measured in a town (seed `a`, software drawing, from five places: in a street, on the quay, on the square looking up and looking out to sea, and over the roofs), the triangles drawn fell by a half to two thirds, and the calls to draw rose by about two fifths. Not done: the house itself is still one mesh to a material (it is baked as one); nothing is left out because it is hidden behind something else; and nothing is drawn more simply for being far off, apart from the vegetation and the far country (a town's buildings carry every pane and moulding, and their rooms inside, at any distance). The sun's shadow is drawn once, not every frame; it is drawn again every thirty frames over the first six hundred, because what the graphics card is not yet ready to draw at the start is left out of it (at an evening hour that had been the hills' long shadow over a whole town).

And *the mirror is drawn only where it is read*: the rectangle of the view that the water's outlines and edges lie in, turned about left for right as the picture is, with room for the ripples, is all of its picture that is painted (`trim` in `water.ts`). By a pond seen across the grass that is a small part of the view; along a brook that runs the length of the valley it is most of it, and saves little. (The cut did nothing at first: the picture is sized afresh every frame just before it is drawn, and sizing it opens all of it again; it is now made after that. Looking across the pond from its bank, where the water is a quarter of the view, a frame then took 11.9 milliseconds against 13.3 without the cut.) And what lies outside the cut is not sent to be drawn at all: the mirror's eye is given a view narrowed to the cut and draws it into that part of the picture alone, which point for point is what the whole view would have drawn there (side by side the two cannot be told apart; a few single points on the edges of blades and leaves differ, by the arithmetic), and its depths are read back through the same narrowing. The eye's view is narrowed by narrowing the main eye's for as long as the mirror takes to copy it, and only then: three also sizes the picture when it builds the water's material, and narrowing it there once left the whole view looking through a keyhole. In the same place the frame then took 11.4 milliseconds; where the pond is a small part of the view and no brook runs across it, most of the mirror's calls to draw go. With all three, and the hollows above added, the same frame takes about 20 milliseconds. Then, in a harbour town's street: every torch, lamp and candle's flame was a mesh with a material of its own, and every smoke over a chimney three sheets each a mesh of its own, and none of them was ever left out for being out of view: 92 calls to draw for smoke and 44 for flames, the flames again in the mirror. In that street the calls to draw fell from 848 to 652, at some thirteen thousandths of a millisecond of the program's time each. Still to do: the main picture's five hundred calls to draw.

- **The space graph is the visibility structure.** Places and ways are rooms and portals, so what cannot be seen is cheaply skipped underground and indoors.
- Static geometry merged per zone; repeated things (props, plants, kit parts, rubble) drawn as instances.
- Levels of detail for terrain, vegetation and distant buildings.
- Generation and baking in background workers so the view stays responsive.
- Textures generated at the resolution the tier calls for.

## Open air: the target and where it stands

The target for outdoor scenes is the look of a well-made open-world game: a wooded valley under a hazy sky, with long grass and flowers underfoot, leafy trees and dark firs, one species in blossom for colour, mossy boulders and outcrops, a worn path, water, and mountains fading into the distance. Two reference pictures supplied for the project show this; they are not kept in the repository.

A broadleaf is grown: a trunk dividing into limbs, branches and twigs, each thinner and shorter than what bears it and turning up towards the light, as real geometry with a bark material; leaves are sprays of cards hung on the twigs, so the crown has the lumps and gaps of the branching under it. Each spray also has a shade of its own: yellower and brighter in the sun at the edge, bluer in the old leaf inside and beneath, with a wander from bough to bough; and on near trees about a fifth of the outermost sprays are left out, so the outline is lumpy and the sky shows through in places (drawn from a stream of its own, so the branching is unchanged). Trunks differ too: each bends or stands straight by its own draw, is fluted at the foot with buttresses between the flutes, or oval, or nearly round (`LimbShape` in `layLimb`). Trees are not all of an age: about one in twenty is a giant, up to twice as tall and stouter; about three in ten are young, slender and low; and very rarely (about one oak in forty of the near ones) an oak of great age has spread rather than risen, with a trunk two and a half times as thick and limbs reaching out low and far (`wide`), a roof over the ground round it. The bark texture has deeper furrows, patches of warmer and cooler brown, round pale lichens in colonies and small yellow-orange flecks. Bushes are small trees of many stems. Conifers are grown too: a straight tapering trunk with branches leaving it in whorls, each carrying sprays of needles laid along it, some flat and some hanging, darker by the trunk and lighter at the tips. A spruce is clothed to the ground, its low boughs long and drooping, its high ones short and rising, every one turned up at the tip; a pine is bare for half its height, with the stubs of shed branches, and has a rounded head of rising boughs. Pines stand in patches among the spruce. Far off, a conifer is still a trunk and tiers of bough cards. Trunks lie where they fell and stumps stand in the shade of the wood. Small wood (roots, bushes, low boughs) is drawn but stops nobody. Still missing: any difference between kinds of leaf, and plants on the cliff ledges. |
| Land running to the horizon | In place: the valley and its rim, which can be walked, a coarse sheet out to 250 metres, and beyond it three ranges of hills and mountains to three kilometres, with ridges, gullies, woods whose trees can be told, pasture and snow, fading into haze. A valley found in worn land has that land for its hills near it, and from about half a kilometre the same ranges rising behind (they had nothing behind the upland's low hills, and the valley stood in a plain to the horizon). Behind its crags the ground goes on up as behind a drawn valley's, under the land it was found in (where the land rose less than the crag stood, the crag's top was left as a level shelf, a quarry's rim). On the nearer hills, crags: tors of the valley's stone, 26 to 48 metres high on a base of 32 to 48, steep at their sides and broad over the top, a heap of blocks rather than a cone (at 42 to 82 metres on 30 to 46 they stood as pyramids). |
| Rock: cliffs, outcrops, boulders, standing stones | In place: see *Cliffs, boulders and scree* above. The cliffs are bedded, stepped and jointed, different for each kind of stone, with scree at the foot. Edges are kept sharp in the carving itself, so ledges, blocks and fracture faces have real corners; nothing smaller than the 30 centimetre lattice can be carved, and below that the texture carries the detail. |
| Leaves lit from behind | In place. Light that has passed the shadow map is let through each card, strongest when the leaf is seen against the sun. |
| A worn earth path, and ground that is more than one texture | Partly. The track down the valley is turf tinted to earth, with no grass on it; the ground has a material of its own, a fine mat with earth in the low places, but still only the one. |
| Water | Still water is a mirror. The scene is drawn a second time as the water would show it, upside down, and laid on the surface, so the far bank, the trees and the mountains stand in the lake; the clouds, which are not things in the scene, are marched again along the reflected line of sight wherever the picture shows open sky. Two sets of ripples bend the picture a little. Water mirrors nearly everything at a glancing angle and little looked straight down on, and at its shallow edge it is clear, so the bed shows: gravel and mud, with rocks half in the water. A lake may have an island with a tree. Running water is the same water, moving. It is a ribbon laid down the course of its stream, falling as the bed falls and reaching on each side as far as the bank stands above it, so its edge is the bank's. It borrows the still water's picture, reading it where its own line of sight would meet the level sheet: far things then stand in it exactly, near ones a little out of place. (A valley with a stream and no pond keeps a mirror for the stream alone, which is never itself drawn.) Its ripples are longer down the current than across and travel down it at two speeds; it is shallow, so the bed shows all the way across: dark wet gravel, with cobbles lying in it and along its edges, which nobody trips on. Where the bed falls steeply the water breaks white. Where a stream meets a pond it comes to the pond's level, quietens, and fades out over the pond's own surface, so there is no line between them; it does the same at its spring and its sink. Low plants, and running water itself, are left out of the mirror's picture, which saves a tenth of the frame. A stream that comes over the rim is a **fall**: a notch is worn in the top of the wall for it, the face under it is worn smooth (the rock keeps almost none of its roughness down that line), and the water is the head of the stream's own ribbon, lying on the rock from a little way back in the notch down to the pool, wherever the rock goes; where the face is in ledges it comes down in leaps. Sheer water is white, in strings and veils that come down fast, and stays white for some way below; three upright sheets of drifting spray stand at the foot. Where the track crosses a brook there are stepping stones (for the eye: the brook is shallow enough to wade), and over a river a timber bridge on two bearers, with posts and rails, which is walked on. Running water takes no shadow. At the lowest level of detail still water mirrors the sky alone, and running water a sky and a dark line of land that are worked out, not seen. |
| Things built outdoors: fences, steps, bridges, jetties | A landing stage and a post-and-rail fence, in plain squared timber. No steps or bridges. |
| Haze and light shafts through the trees | Haze is in place. The fog does not yet know about the trees, so there are no shafts between them. |
| Keeping the cost down | Cards are sorted into blocks of ground so that those out of view are not drawn, and grass and flowers shrink away and stop being drawn beyond a distance set by the level of detail (40, 55 or 80 metres). Three levels of detail, chosen on the opening card, also set the pixel ratio, texture size, bake rays, bloom and the share of leaf cards drawn. Distant trees have fewer branches and fewer, larger cards. No impostors. On a desktop graphics card the frame rate sits at the browser's cap; a tablet has not been tried with the present scene. |

Nothing below the trees is scattered at random. The lie of the land is reduced to four numbers at every point, each from 0 to 1, and every plant is placed by them.

### The island

An island (`?place=island`) is the other kind of open place, and the target is a different picture: a high green island in a grey-blue sea, ringed with cliffs that stand straight out of the water, stacks off its headlands, a cove with a beach and a few white cottages, a lighthouse on the far point, and a long coast of hills across the water. The western isles of Scotland and Ireland, the Skelligs, the north coast of Cornwall.

| Ingredient | How it is made |
|---|---|
| Sea cliffs | As the valley's cliffs are: beds, frost wearing on a lattice of heights, then carving with the beds stepped and split into blocks. The plain shape falls from the cliff top to a sea bed some metres down over a few strides, so nearly all of it is carved as rock. Below a few metres under the water nothing is carved: the sea is too deep to see into there. The lattice is 40 centimetres, coarser than the valley's 30, because there is far more cliff. |
| Rock by the sea | Banded as real sea cliffs are: under the water dark and furred; between the tides hung with olive wrack; above that a band of black lichen as deep as the spray reaches; higher, orange and grey-green lichens in patches on what takes the weather, and white under the ledges near the top where the birds sit. The smaller breaks are scoured off where the waves reach. |
| Stacks and boulders | Pillars of the same rock standing out of the sea off the headlands, made as part of the land's heights and carved with it; and forty-odd blocks fallen from the cliffs lying at their foot half in the water, made as the valley's boulders are and coloured with the sea's bands, so the surf has something to break on. |
| Relief | No plateau: summits steep and broken (a cone hollowed and gullied by ridged noise), and wherever ground is steeper than about forty degrees it is carved as rock, as the sea cliffs are, so the summits stand in crags. Round the coast the land comes down to a low rocky shore in some stretches and stands at full height in others. |
| The stair in the cliff | On islands without a beach: a ramp at one in two cut into the face of the cove's cliff, a stride or so in from the face so the rock stands sheer behind it and drops in front, made as part of the land's heights so the carving keeps it clean (no broken rock on it, no frost wearing at it); on it, a step every half metre cut in the cliff's own rock, each coloured as the cliff is at that place and height (its beds, the tide's dark bands low down, the lichens higher), not quite square, its nose worn round and its tread paler down the middle where feet have gone; for the eye, as on the valley's terraces. (They were blocks of pale building stone, which looked laid on the rock: "the stairs dont look like theyve been carved from the same stone as the cove".) The quay and the jetty are built of the island's stone too, coloured as the rock about them. The ledge either side of the steps and the floor they stand on are the rock they are cut in, never ground: drawn as ground they were turf, and so near the sea that was sand, a band of yellow up the cliff beside the steps. |
| Beach | At the head of the cove the land comes down to the water through a hollow, and the bare ground there is sand: the turf's material, with a sand texture in place of beaten earth, darker where it is wet. |
| The sea | A sheet in rings about the island to 3.4 kilometres, each point of which knows how deep the water is under it. It is not a mirror: three trains of swell, half a metre high, lift and tilt the surface itself (less in the shallows, and not at all far out, where the rings are too far apart to carry it), so the water rises and falls against the cliffs and runs up the sand; and two sizes of waves on the swell break the sky's reflection into glints, finer and fainter with distance. It reflects less than a still pond would at a glancing angle, since its waves hide part of what it would show, and the sky in it is brought down to no brighter than the haze, or a sea at noon reads as snow. It is clear and green over sand, dark blue-green where deep. Where it shoals it breaks in lines that run in to the shore, and against every cliff foot and stack there is a fringe of foam; out on the open water a crest breaks white now and then. |
| Turf | Short and burnt yellow-grey by salt towards the cliff's edge, greener in the shelter of the hollow, brown under heather; and in broad patches, tens of metres across, rough grazing (moor grass and rush, olive and brown) between the sweeter green the sheep keep short. Without them the island from above was one even green, like a lawn. |
| Bare earth | Peat over thin soil, dark and brown (`far.bare` in `island.ts`): the track and the footpaths worn into the turf are that colour. With the valley's pale soil, in the open sun of an island the track read as chalk and a footpath across the turf as a white line. |
| What grows | Decided by the wind (see `islandPlanting.ts`). On a found island the shelter is read from the land: a hollow, with ground higher between it and the nearest sea; trees grow only there, few and low and swept away from the sea; bracken and foxgloves follow the same shelter; gorse grows in thickets tens of metres across. Before: short turf burnt by salt at the cliff edge, with thrift and sea campion in it; heath of heather and gorse in from the edge; marram on the sand; bracken, foxgloves and low trees only in the shelter of the hollow; a windbreak of pines beside the house. Thrift, marram and gorse are new cards. |
| Spray | Where the swell runs against a cliff or a stack it bursts up the rock: an upright sheet of drifting white at each such place, rising as the swell there peaks and thinning as it falls back, timed from the same trains of waves that lift the water. |
| Air | The mist lies on the sea, not on the house's floor twenty metres above it: a sea fog in the morning and at dusk, with the island standing out of it. |
| Rain | From the weather menu or `?rain=` (0 to 1), in any place: heavy cloud low and in banks, thicker air and some mist, the sun dimmed behind it. Streaks falling about whoever looks, in a box of air thirty metres across that goes with them, slanted by the wind and each as long as it falls in a moment (`rain.ts`, worked out where it is drawn); not drawn under a roof, and thinning and thickening over a moment going in and out. | Sheep | Two to four flocks of four to nine on the island's pasture (well in from the cliffs, off the track and paths, away from the house, cottages and lighthouse, a good way apart), and one or two of three to eight on a valley's open ground (out of the woods, off the track, away from the house and the water). | Fireflies | In a valley on a spring or summer evening (dusk and night), not in rain: up to seven swarms of a hundred or two, half of them by the streams and the rest along the edges of the woods, low over the grass (most at the height of the grass heads, a few up to two metres; over water, above it), recorded by the mesher whatever the hour (`desc.fireflies`). ### The mountain top

A mountain top (`?place=mountain`) is the third kind of open place. The target: a summit of bare rock standing out of a sea of white cloud in sun, other peaks rising out of it all round, a path along a ridge coming up out of the cloud to a stone hospice in a saddle; in winter all of it under snow but the steep faces. The Alps above an inversion; the Tatras; a hospice on a pass.

| Element | How it is made |
|---|---|
| Rock | As the island's cliffs, carved round the rim, the ridge's edges and wherever the top is steep (the summit's horn, the crags by the landings, the ridge the climb runs on), worn harder by frost (more passes, more debris left lying). No sea bands and no moss: lichens only, yellow-green map lichen in patches, rusty orange, and black streaks where melt water runs. Nothing is carved below what the cloud hides. |
| Ground | Thin brown turf in the hollows and on the gentler slopes, gravel and stone between, barer the higher it stands. Seen from afar the turf takes the colour of what grows on it, which up here is dry and brown, not a meadow's green (`groundUniforms.uSward`). The way is a worn path; nothing on wheels comes here. |
| Snow | In the materials (`snowOn` in `siteMaterial.ts`), on rock, ground and, once the bake has said what sees the sky, what is built: laid on whatever faces up enough to hold it, with ragged edges from noise at two sizes, lying first in the cracks of the stone, rippled by the wind on the ground at three sizes (long swells of drift, ridges a stride or two apart cut by the wind, and ripples a hand apart near the eye), with a glint here and there where a grain catches the sun, packed grey on the paths; and a little under fresh snow's whiteness, since in a clear sun anything brighter was drawn as flat white. `uSnow` is how much (winter 1, summer a little) and `uSnowLine` a level above which there is more, so that in summer what is left lies high up. |
| The sea of cloud | Drawn with the air (`post.ts`), not as a surface: its top is a field of billows at three sizes, from a mile across to a couple of hundred metres, drifting with the wind, with heads that tower where a swell and a heap coincide, and smaller knobs seen only near the eye. Its heaps shade one another: towards the sun, a little way off and further, is the cloud higher than the sun's light comes in at? No mist lies over it (the cloud is the mist) and the haze above it is under half a valley's. The mountain's shadow lies on it (see `uShadeBody`, `uShadePeak`), miles long under a low sun. And it may be broken (`uDeck`'s fourth number, `uDeckGap`): where a slow noise clears a bar the cloud's top sinks away in walls and thins to nothing, and the land under it shows; never near the mountain, which stands in the cloud, nor further off than land is drawn. A line of sight from above is walked down to it; what it meets is lit as cloud (white towards the sun, never dark facing away, the sky's colour in its troughs, a bright edge against the sun) and whatever lies beyond is hidden over a few tens of metres, so rock going down into it fades and is not cut at a line. Seen from inside, it is cloud all round, thinner towards its top. The haze is reckoned only as far as the cloud, so far off it fades into the sky's colour at the horizon. On all that, heaps (asked for: it read as "very two dimensional disc like"): domes some hundreds of metres across standing five or six billows (fifty metres or so) over the sea, rounded over the top and falling steeply between, with smaller heads on them, read from the same noise as the swells; banked up against the mountain's sides, as cloud driven against a cliff is; and none over the ridge the way comes up out of the cloud by (`uDeckSpur`), so the player is not kept in the fog. Over the tops lies a fringe a few metres deep, thinning upward, gathered along the line of sight, so the heaps' edges are soft; taken as deep as one step of the walk falls and none over a break, or it showed as ripples. The walk is 38 steps. Seen whole from the air it costs about 2 milliseconds of a frame (it was about 1 before the heaps, estimated). |
| The peaks round about | One sheet in rings to 6.9 kilometres (from a mountain the eye is let see 7.4), drawn with the far material: horns with hollow flanks and three or four ridges, each great one a massif with lesser summits about it, the nearer ones half a kilometre off and a great range of giants beyond, all standing on a hidden country under the cloud; how high each point stands over the cloud is what the far material reads for its snow: all white over the cloud in winter, only the high peaks in summer. On worn land each great horn's faces are planes cut back by the ice each a distance of its own and turned a little from an even pyramid, hollowed and steeper towards the summit; down each face run couloirs a hundred metres or so apart, fading towards the summit, the arêtes and the foot; the arêtes are notched by towers and gaps; and across the faces lie ledges. Snow on what is too steep to hold it still lodges in the gullies and on broken ledges of the beds, so a steep face high up is dark rock streaked and banded with white. (Before, each face was one smooth plane, and the peaks far off read as pyramids of glass.) On those faces, laid on as the land is read and after all the wearing (worn with the rest, the frost, which knocks back what is steeper than such faces already stand, rubbed it away), the rock: buttresses and ribs standing out and gullies cut between them, some tens of metres deep and fifty to a hundred and fifty apart, drawn out down the fall line and wandering so that they join and part, in up to four octaves near and one far off; and the arêtes broken into towers and gaps (`hornRelief` in `mountain.ts`). Measured across a face, relief finer than forty metres went from about 3 metres to about 6, finer than a hundred and fifty from 15 to 20 or 40. The sheet's rings lie about as close as the points round them (a point every 15 metres round and a ring every 19 out, at three kilometres; it was 40 out, so a face looking this way was drawn in rows forty metres apart): 1.1 million triangles where there were 0.6, about 0.8 milliseconds a frame. And it lies in its own shadows: the sun's shadow map covers only the site, so out here no ridge threw a shadow on anything; the sun does not move, so from each point over the cloud a ray is followed towards it once, at build, over the sheet's own heights, softer the further off what it meets, and the far material keeps the sun off as much of each point as that says. None of the great ones stands within about 1.1 kilometres of the top, or its faces would reach the top's own ground and be drawn as its plain sheet. The far sheet has 1,792 points round each ring and its rings as close (about 10 metres at three kilometres): 2.1 million triangles, about 2.3 milliseconds of a frame seen whole from the air, and 3 seconds of the build. And finer than any sheet, on steep faces of the heights, the far material draws the rock as light and shade, and as where snow can lie: gullies some twenty metres apart and ribs some seven running down the face, ledges where the beds come out (dipping, of all thicknesses, coming and going along each), and the chips of the rock, each size kept while it is four or five points of the picture across. |

| Rock faces | Frost-shattered: the stone's character is taken sharper and blockier on a mountain (`rockKind` in `mountain.ts`, and `rockSharp` for the material). The broad relief is fracture planes (`planes`): a lattice of points nine metres apart and thirteen tall, each with a plane tilted its own way, mostly upright; the face at a point is the highest of the planes near it, so it is flat faces meeting in edges. Chimneys are cut down it where a cell's edge falls among one in three of them. All of that is reckoned on a lattice a metre and a half apart and blended between; the fine relief (beds, joints, blocks) is the valley's, on the sharper character. |
| Snowy faces, gullies and finer planes (20 November 2026) | Asked for more detail in the play area, said to look made of polygons, and then about the stepped look of the slopes, like a model of their contours. The beds of the rock were cut into ledges on all rock alike, on a slope of forty degrees as on a cliff, every ledge the same height (two beds, some 1.2 metres) and running unbroken round the hill, with snow on each; the faces were planes of one size, nine metres, so seen from a stride off a face was a few flat sheets; and nothing ran down a face to cut its ledges. | Paths | Never straight: the way follows the ridge's swinging crest and wanders on it; the climb is a curve through points out on the top; the gallery way follows the face. Kerb stones on edge along both sides; treads of split rock where the grade is a stair's, no face of each slab square, its top drawn in a little at each corner by its own amount and its nose worn back and down (cut as boxes they read as poured concrete); on scree the path is a trodden band, darker and finer (`footWorn` in the ground material). |
| Needles, tors and stones | Needles: towers of rock standing out of the cloud off the precipices, each a skin about its axis, ring upon ring, drawn with the rock's own material and colour; not carved, since nobody goes near them. Their section is a prism of five to seven faces (the joints), turning a little as they rise, ribbed up their height, stepped in at two to five heights on one side only where blocks have fallen, with faint beds and a head broken into points. (They were first drawn with a ledge all round at every bed, evenly spaced, and in winter each ledge held a ring of snow: they were taken for pagodas.) Tors, teeth and the crags by the landings: stacks of blocks (`raiseStack` in `render/mountain.ts`; 20 November 2026). They were bumps of the land, its height raised in a knob, a notched fin or a tower and rising in steps a bed thick, so their sides were smooth curved walls banded where the height stepped: a turned post, not rock. Now the land is laid without them (`landAt(..., bare)` in the core) and each is carved by itself, as a boulder is, as blocks one on another, each a prism of six to nine faces, uneven, most leaning back a little and some forward, with a top tilted a little, its edges worn a little round and its skin broken by the rock's own joints and smaller planes (with flat faces alone a stack was a pillar of dressed stone), the next narrower and set a little aside; a tooth is a blade, longer one way than the other, a tor a squat knob two or three blocks broad at its foot, a crag a tower. The snow lies on each block's top. Carved on a lattice a ninth of its breadth (a fifth to a third of a metre), sunk into the ground so that no gap shows at its foot; on Ben Bran they add some 120,000 triangles (1.48 million of rock in all, against 1.76 before the faces were reworked) and no time to a frame that could be measured, and plants and boulders keep off them. Stones: rough blocks of a few faces lying in drifts, thickest under crags and on bare ground. Bare ground itself is talus, tinted to the mountain's rock (`groundUniforms.uBare`): a texture painted for mountains (`talus`), each piece a cell with a tilted flat top and a sharp edge, where the valley's scree is rounded lumps. |
| The gallery way | Its ledges are the land's shape (cut in and built out to the way's level, as the island's stair is); its tunnels and their windows are air carved out of the rock by the builder, the bore following the way's own level; the rock about them is kept from wearing and from the breaks that shift a face, or the tunnel's side opens. Inside, the rock is darkened by how far it is from a mouth or a window, and torches burn on the inner wall. Posts and a rail of iron stand wherever the ground drops beside an open stretch. |
| Cornices | Winter only. A solid of snow carved with the rock (`cornice` in `mountain.ts`): along the rim on the side away from the wind, where the precipice stands high and no path or tunnel comes near, a lip out over the edge as far as the ring of reaches says, its top rising to the brink from what the worn rock is a stride and a half in, its underside curling back to the face. Every point of it is marked for snow (the rock's third field less than nothing), so it is white underneath as on top. Fences stand at the brink as before, so nobody walks out onto one. |
| Snow on the steps | Winter only. Each tread of the stairs up the ridge, the climb and the gallery way carries a cushion of snow (`cushion` in `mountain.ts`): a closed body rounded down to nothing at every edge, some ten to twenty centimetres deep and deeper towards the back, where it drifts against the next riser, a little uneven, its foot sunk into the slab so that it meets the worn nose without a gap. (The snow had been the material's alone, laid on whatever faces up, and a tread was a stone block with a white top and no snow on it at all; asked for on 19 November 2026.) The steps are for the eye, so the snow stops no foot. |
| The scramble | A path up an arête: the land built into a sharp ridge along its line (falling at 0.78 of the distance to the power 1.3, where the climb's ridge falls at 0.5), footholds of rock here and there, and iron stanchions with a chain hanging in four sagging lengths from each to the next, on one hand only. |

The cleft under it is the land's shape. The bell tower is coursed blocks in three lifts with string courses, an open stage of four piers, a bell in three rings of bronze, and four slopes of slate to a point. |

Torches in brackets down the hall's walls, along the gallery and in the side halls; what is built inside sees little of the sky and is lit so. ### The town

A town (`?place=town`) is the fourth kind of place: a harbour town in a bay between headlands, the mountains standing over it, as the small ports of the northern coasts stand, and the picture to aim at is what is seen from the head of the mole on arriving: the harbour's still water, the waterfront of stuccoed classical fronts in pale yellow, rose and green, the house on its terrace at the head of the square, timber-framed and boarded houses climbing behind under red tiles and dark metal, a row of red storehouses along the shore, fields on the plain and woods going up into the hills.

| Ingredient | How it is made |
|---|---|
| The land | Found, not drawn: a coast country of eight kilometres is made by noise (a coast wandering in broad bays and in points of hard rock that stand high, low land rising inland, hills, then mountains of a thousand metres and more) and worn by water and frost (`coastland.ts`); a shore is searched for with open water before it for a harbour, low ground behind it rising gently, the house's ground a few metres over the square's, country climbing behind, and better still a headland beside it and land standing out either side, so that the harbour lies in a bay. The town's own land (nine hundred metres by five hundred and fifty) is that country made fine on a lattice of a metre and a half and worn again; then the sea cuts it back along the coast by a few metres to thirty, which leaves cliffs where the land rose steeply from the water and only a lost strip of shore where it was low, and nothing along the harbour. Then the town is laid on it, and the land made over only where the town needs it: graded under the blocks and streets (the land smoothed over twenty-five metres, never lower than a little over the quay), the square and the waterfront laid to their own falls, the house's terrace level, level ground under every building, the harbour dug out, quays made up behind their walls. |
| Rock | Wherever the made land is steep, which is the sea's cliffs, the headland's crags and the hills', it is carved as the island's cliffs are, in tiles: beds, frost wear, faces stepped and split, the sea's bands of weed and lichen at their foot. Everything else is one sheet of ground. |
| Paving | In the ground's own material, so that it follows every rise and fall of a street without a seam: the sheet carries in its first field how far in from the paving's edge each point is (setts in a street, flags on the square, the waterfront and the forecourt), and the material draws the stones, each its own tone, the joints dark, the edges of setts rounded in the light; further off the pattern fades into its own average. Flags are laid in courses each of its own length of stone, as a quarry gives them, every stone its own tone and a warmer or cooler cast; close by each has a grain of its own and a mottle across it (old wet and lichen on some, scrubbed pale on others), and lies not quite level, so that the sun catches the stones unevenly; the joints hold dirt, and moss where feet go least. (Without the grain and the tilt a quay seen from the eye's height was a floor of tiles.) A garden's walks are gravel, the shingle read small and pale, its relief read from the heights the shingle's picture carries a step either way (not from a normal map of its own: one more texture took the ground's material to seventeen, past the sixteen a stage may sample under WebGPU, which then drew no level ground at all, in any kind of place). Every kind of paving carries how far in it is in one band of the field's first number, and what kind it is (setts, flags or gravel) in the litter channel, which paving has no other use for: when the kinds were told apart by bands of the first number, the sheet's triangles, blending the flags' number down to nothing where they meet grass, passed through the setts' band and drew a strip of setts in the grass a few metres off every flagged edge. Wet, it shines. **Laid by the street** (19 November 2026, asked how paved and tiled surfaces and roads could be made more real). The setts had been one grid of identical rectangles laid along x over the whole town, whichever way a street ran, with blocky mottling from noise read on a square lattice; the square read as a floor of tiles. Now the town works out, on its lattice of a metre, which way its paving is laid (`PaveFrame`, made in `buildTown` from each street's middle line made smooth and cut into half-metre lengths, so that courses fan round a bend; the square and the forecourt square to themselves, the waterfront's courses along the quay): how far along and across its street each point is, the way the street runs there, and which street it is. The ground's material reads that without a filter (`paveFrameAt`: a float texture loaded point by point, so it costs no sampler; only where the graphics card allows twenty textures or more to a stage, `?paveframe=0` to leave it out), carries each of the four lattice points about it on to the point the way its street runs, and mixes those of the nearest point's street, so that where two streets meet their courses meet along a line, as they do. Setts lie in courses across the street, of granite of the fields, grey, pink, red-brown, blue-grey and pale stone by stone; a street in two of every four has its courses laid in arcs two metres between springings. Neither courses nor stones are all one size, each course begins its stones at its own place, the corners are rounded and the edges a little ragged, as split stone is (`stonesAt`, `cellsOf`, `stoneAt`). Joints between setts are a finger broad and filled with sand and grit, moss where feet go least and towards the walls; tops are rubbed paler where feet go. Flags are of a few beds of one quarry, chipped at their corners, settled each its own way, with lichen in round spots where few feet go (`spotsOf`) and one in eleven cracked across. Within some ten metres the paving has depth: the picture is slid along the line of sight until the line meets a stone (eight steps and one between), and from there the sun is followed a little way (four steps), so that a joint lies in the shadow of the stone on its sunward side (`stoneHeight`; the joint's shadow goes into the sun's light as `jointLit`). The noise is read through a plane turned a little more than a third of a right angle (`rot`), so that it shows none of the squares it is made on. All of it is worked out only where there is paving. Looking down at a street of setts that filled the view, at midday, a frame took about 11.8 milliseconds at 1024 by 768 on the desktop against 11.3 for a street of earth, which has its own depth; without the joints' shadow, about 10.7. A street seen along its length took about 16. |
| The harbour | A quay of dressed blocks in courses, dark and weeded below the tide, coped with long stones, with iron bollards and flights of steps let into its face; moles from its ends, battered, decked with flags, a parapet along the seaward side and rough blocks heaped against its foot. Their form is the coast's: how far the land stands out to sea beyond each end of the quay, within three hundred metres, tells a bay (land standing out on both hands), a headland on one hand, or an open coast. In a bay, which shelters it already, two straight piers, leaning in or side by side, or short arms, or a breakwater; where a headland stands on one hand, one long breakwater from the open side, curving or bent square across the harbour's front, with a short pier from the other end; on an open coast, two arms curving towards each other, or such a breakwater, or a breakwater standing off in the sea before the harbour with a mouth at each end and a short pier from each end of the quay (its ends both rounded off). The harbour's water is the outline of the quay, the moles and the mouths between them. (Every harbour had been the same two arms.) On the head of the mole the player arrives on (always one joined to the quay), a round light, whitewashed and banded, with a gallery and a lantern whose beam turns by night. Vessels lie along the quay and ride with the swell: where the quay is long enough a two-masted schooner of twenty to twenty-five metres, black above a band of colour and red below the water, with a deckhouse, a hatch, bulwarks, a bowsprit, booms with their sails furled and stays to the mastheads; two or three single-masted smacks of nine to thirteen metres; and open boats in what room is left and out in the harbour. Along the back of the quay lie heaps of what has been landed: crates (some stacked), barrels standing and lying, sacks and heaped nets. Beside the ship a crane of timber on a block of stone: a mast with a jib raked up from low on it out over the water, held by a rope from the mast's head, two shores behind, a winch at its foot, and a crate in a sling hanging over the ship's deck. (A level arm with a barrel hanging from it was tried first, and it looked like a gallows.) |
| The square and the house's terrace | The cathedral (see *The cathedral* below) stands on its terrace at the head of the square, its west front to it, its forecourt flagged; a wall of dressed stone with a balustrade of turned balusters runs along the terrace's front, and a broad stair comes down between cheeks into the square. The square is flagged, with a fountain in its middle (a basin, a column, a bowl, a figure's plinth) and two rows of market stalls under striped awnings either side of the way down from the stair, each with its wares. Benches of stone and oak along the square's sides; on its axis towards the harbour, an obelisk of the country's stone on stepped plinths, with a gilt ball on its point; and lime trees in a row down either side of the square and along the back of the waterfront, before the fronts. |
| The house's garden | Behind the house, on the open ground between the blocks either side of the square's line, wherever that is lawn and no steeper than about one in seven: walks of gravel on a square grid of thirteen metres struck from the house's middle and from a walk along its back, laid only where they lead round a bed; between them beds, only in pairs either side of the house's line so that the garden is as symmetrical as the house (laid one-sided where the ground let it, it looked abandoned half made), bordered by low clipped hedges (a green core under box leaves set close and large over its faces, following the ground's rise; with fewer and smaller leaves it was a black box with sprigs on it) with a gap in the middle of each side; every other bed bedded out with flowers in rows, two colours to a bed by turns, each plant a clump of leaves with its flowers over it, set close so that the bed is a carpet of colour on dug earth; and the rest mown lawn with a clipped cone in its middle; tall cones of yew at the corners of the beds either side of the main walk; and a sundial on a stone baluster where four beds meet nearest the middle. Nothing is levelled for it, and it is the mesher's, from the seed, not the document's. (That ground had been one lawn climbing behind the house, the largest bare thing in the town seen from above.) |
| The plan | Each town is laid on a plan of its own kind, and its own measures. Its streets are lines through points, a line along the shore at every row of blocks and one up from the shore at every column, and the plan moves the points: `planned`, square and straight, a town laid out at one stroke; `shore`, its rows following the coast beyond the quay (the coast found where the land meets the water, smoothed over some eighty metres and held to a slope of one in three), so that the town bends with its bay; `fan`, its columns spreading as they climb from the harbour, a few degrees more at each, and its rows bowed back from the water towards their ends, as an amphitheatre's are about its stage; `old`, every street a little off the line of the next and every crossing moved a few metres, as a town grows that was never planned. Besides: how large its blocks are (twenty-six to forty metres, or long ones of forty to fifty-five), how broad its streets, how many blocks of its classical quarter line the waterfront and the square (one to three each), and how far its timber quarter reaches (two to four columns, three to five rows), within so much frontage in all, nearest the square first. The square, the waterfront along the quay and the house's ground are the same in every plan. (Every town had been the same grid, five columns either side of the square and six rows back from the water, told apart only by which blocks the land refused.) |
| The blocks | The blocks between the streets built up round their edges with their yards within; where a block's corner is not square, the houses along its sides keep out of it by as much as the other side's houses stand in it. The blocks by the square and along the waterfront are classical (see *Variations of the classical building*: half of them one broad building with wings down the side streets, and every closed block entered by a carriage way; and about one in three of them of bare brown brick, either with its dressings stuccoed white over a stuccoed, rusticated ground storey, or brick all over, dressings and all, laid a shade paler as moulded brick is. The brick is a texture of its own, running bond of bricks twenty-two centimetres by seven and a half, each its own brown, now and then one burnt dark or fired pale, in recessed pale mortar, with streaks where rain runs down); further out they are timber-framed houses, gable or eaves to the street, with a classical house of wood among them now and then and, on a corner, one built as an L. Every building is made in its own measures by the valley's builder, then turned to its street and set on its ground, on a footing of stone where the street falls away along its front. Iron lamps stand along the streets by turns on either side, round the square and along the quay's edge; their glass is a window's, and glows after dark as a lit window does, though it throws no light on the street. Smoke rises from a chimney here and there (each from one of the chimneys its builder put on the roof), and from the stovepipes of some of the shacks. In the yards are gardens: a fruit tree or two, beds of vegetables in rows, flowers along the walls. |
| The ways | What each street is laid with follows the quarter it serves: flags beside the classical quarter, whose streets are straight however the rest of the town is laid (the crossings about its blocks are never moved); setts or beaten earth among the timber houses, each line of street its own, more earth further out and in an old town most of all; earth or gravel for the lanes through the shacks. Beaten earth in a street is trodden bare across its breadth, not two ruts in grass as a track in the fields is. (Every street had been setts.) A Hanseatic house, when there is one, will stand where the timber houses do. |
| Canal houses | Along the river's quay the blocks' fronts are canal houses, wall against wall, whatever the block's quarter: as on the canals of Amsterdam, a front of a single window beside a double house of five or seven, gables of every outline, stoops out onto the quay. Two towns in five are Dutch, as the harbours of the Zuiderzee are: their waterfront is built of canal houses too, and so is every side of the classical quarter's blocks but the one that faces the square, which keeps its classical front. A Dutch town has fifty to a hundred and more of them; a town with only its river, five to fifteen. A block built all round with canal houses has no carriage way into its yard, as the canal blocks have none. They can be entered (see *Inside the houses of brick and the canal houses*). |
| The river | In about two towns in five a river comes down out of the hills through the open ground behind the house and, bending round the back of the house's terrace, runs straight down beside the square into the harbour. In the town it runs between walls of dressed stone from its bed up to quays along each bank, coped, with an iron railing along the top; at the foot of the walls, on one bank or both, a boardwalk of timber on posts in the water, with a rail on the water's side and a stair down to it from the quay, in stretches between the bridges. Its level steps down to the sea in weirs, as high as its banks allow (nearly two metres under the quays in the town, about one under the banks in the hills), and the water is white where it falls. Above the town it runs between banks of earth sloping down to it, narrowing into the hills, swinging from side to side and taking the lowest way. Wherever a street, a track or a path crosses it there is a bridge at the way's own level: in the town of stone, a segmental arch springing a little above the water between abutments, a moulded ring about the arch, the deck flagged and walled along its sides; out of it of timber, on trestles, with handrails. The waterfront crosses its mouth by the broadest bridge, and the quay's face is open there. That side's blocks stand back across the far quay, and the classical ones front the river and the square beyond it. |

Where the shore will not take them, shacks stand along it instead, some on posts at the water's edge. |

### The cathedral

A harbour town's house is a cathedral (`src/core/cathedral.ts` plans it, `src/render/cathedral.ts` builds its outside; its rooms are built as any house's are). The picture to aim at is the one from the head of the mole: over the waterfront's roofs a great church at the head of the square, its west towers and spires higher than anything in the town, the clerestory standing over the aisles on flying buttresses, a forest of pinnacles; and from the square, the west front filling the view.

| Ingredient | How it is made |
|---|---|
| How high | Measured against the town, once the town is laid out round the church's ground: the nave's ridge is between one and a half and three times as high as the tallest building within 170 metres of the forecourt (to its ridge, reckoned generously), dealt for each seed. The aisles rise to about half the nave's walls; the clerestory has at least four metres of window over the aisles' roofs. In the towns seen so far the ridge is 38 to 74 metres and the towers' tops at it or a fifth over it, their spires higher again. |
| Not stone by stone | Every surface the house's builder lays (walls, vaults, arches, ribs, floors, boards and beams) is laid flat: one plain surface cut on a lattice of 0.35 to 1.1 metres, fine enough to carry the baked light and to follow an opening's edge, which the dressed band round each opening covers (`MasonryStyle.flat`). The courses and joints are in the picture, `ashlar`: cut stone in courses of 0.4 metres, each course cut into blocks at joints of its own so that no two break joint alike, each block a tone of its own (paler, darker, warmer, cooler, now and then one with iron in it), the face dressed with the drag of the tool, the arrises worn round and chipped, joints of lime a centimetre broad standing back. The stone's tint is the country's, as everything built of it. What is carved (tracery, pinnacles, crockets, spires, figures, the portal's orders) is plain solids of dressed stone, as the towns' buildings are made. |
| Walls | Traced from the outline of the church's places, each grown by a wall, in bands of height: in each band, the outline of every place that stands at least that high (to its vault's crown and a fill over it), traced exactly on the lines of their edges, and a face of wall up every stretch of it, carried up through the bands for as long as it goes on. So the clerestory stands over the aisles' roofs and the towers out of everything, with no face built where another place's wall stands against it. A plinth along the foot, broken for the doors, and a moulded course under the top of each stretch. |
| Windows | Worked out from what of each wall is seen: from what hides it (the places beyond it to their tops, and their roofs where they come against it) up to under its vault, or in an end wall up into the vault's lunette. The aisles' windows at every bay, the clerestory's over them; three or four to a transept's side; in a transept's end a tall window of lights and over it, in the lunette, a round one; in the choir's east end over the shrine's roof a round window or a tall one; the west front's over the portal and its gallery. A pointed window has two to four lights under pointed heads of their own, a ring with four cusps in the head over them, mullions, transoms every three metres, iron bars; a round one a ring, a hub, spokes and a ring of roundels. Coloured glass within, in panes of a little over half a metre (deep blue and ruby in bands, a gold border, green and purple among them), glowing with the day; dark glass without. A hood moulding over each. |
| Buttresses | At every bay of the church's walls and its outer corners, in three stages, each finished with a sloping weathering and the last with a pinnacle: a square shaft, a pyramid with crockets up its edges, a finial. Over the aisles, from each buttress along their long sides, a pier carried up over the aisle's roof and from it a flying buttress, a half arch to the clerestory where the vault's thrust comes, under a coping, at about a third of a right angle. |
| Roofs | Steep (a rise of 1.25 to 1.7 over the half breadth) over the nave and the choir, as one ridge from the west front to the east end unless a tower stands over the crossing, and over each transept's arm into the main roof; lean-tos over the aisles against the clerestory, at one in two; the shrine's lower, its gable to the east; a pyramid over the chapter house with a gilt finial; hipped roofs of red tiles over the sacristy, refectory and library. Lead (grey), copper gone green or dark glazed tiles, the metal with standing seams every two thirds of a metre; a parapet along every eave of the church; gables of stone where an end is seen, coped up both slopes, kneelers at their feet, a cross of stone at the head, pinnacles at the shoulders of the great ones and a small round window high in them. |
| Towers | Buttressed at their seen corners by pairs set square to each face, stepping back in four stages to under the parapet; string courses at a third and two thirds of the way to the belfry and at its floor; on every face it shows, stages of blind lancets (pointed panels of darker stone sunk in a moulding, mullioned) wherever there is no window; lancets lighting the stair inside wherever its flight leaves room on that wall; the belfry's paired (or, on a broad tower, three) tall openings behind louvres on every face but the one its stair rises against; a parapet with a coping; a great pinnacle at each corner. Over that a spire: of stone, eight-sided, crocketed up its edges and banded, with four lucarnes at its foot, a finial and an iron cross; or of lead or of copper with a gilt ball and cross; or none (one of two towers is now and then left without, as happened). |
| The crossing | A tower over it in some: louvred openings for bells on every face over the roofs' ridge, buttressed corners with pinnacles, a parapet, and a spire or none. Otherwise a flèche on the ridge: an open drum of eight posts and a needle of the roofs' metal. |
| The west front | The portal: a block standing forward of the front, cut through by a pointed arch, and in it four orders stepping in to the doors, each with a shaft and a capital at the springing; a tympanum between the innermost order's point and the doors' round head with a figure seated in a mandorla; over all a gable to a finial, coped and crocketed, pinnacles either side. Over that the west window or the rose, and the nave's gable between the towers. |

## 2D maps

Maps are assets in their own right, for use in games, on virtual tabletops and in print, and they double as the in-tool navigation aid.

| Style | Description | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Plan** | Clean technical drawing with optional overlays for zones, critical path, locks, fields and metrics | The workbench; debugging |
| **Ink** | Hand-drawn look: wobbly line, hatching around walls, simple symbols; and a second sheet of the whole place, valley, island, mountain top or town, with hachures down its falls, the sea in lines, cloud in banks. A town's: its fields hatched as they are grown (grain in close lines, roots and vines in dashed rows, an orchard in dots), its streets stippled as they are laid, its buildings outlined and hatched (classical close, the rest more openly), the square flagged with its stalls, fountain and stair, the river between its banks with its flow, weirs and bridges, the harbour's water in ripples with its boats, and names: the square, the harbour, the river, the outlook, the hall | Printable adventure maps |
| **Illustrated** | Parchment, shaded relief, drawn trees and roofs, labels | Handouts, site overviews |
| **Rendered** | Top-down orthographic render of the 3D scene with its lighting, roofs and ceilings removed | Battle maps for tabletop play |
| **Automap** | Minimal, revealed as explored | In the explorer |

Common to all styles:

- One sheet per storey (cellars, ground, upper floors, rooftops), plus a site overview for open-air sites with relief shading and contours.
- Layers that can be toggled and exported separately: ground, walls, objects, water, vegetation, grid, labels, secrets.
- A numbered key generated from the site document: each place's name, fiction and contents.
- Output as vector and raster images, with an optional grid at a chosen scale.
- Optional export of walls, doors and lights in a form virtual tabletops can import.

## Where the look stands

A first scene exists: a walled courtyard with an arcade and a well, and a doorway into a barrel-vaulted hall lit by four torches. It is assembled by hand from the kit of parts, which is the code the site mesher will call later.

| Proof | State |
|---|---|
| Procedural material with normal, roughness and height, blended by a field | Stone, wood and moss recipes; moss, damp and soot driven by fields and by the bake |
| Baked indirect light computed in the browser | Working, in web workers, shown progressively |
| Sun shadows, and shadowed local lights | Working; all shadow maps are drawn once because the world is static |
| Fog with visible shafts of light | Working, by marching view rays against a sun depth map |
| Target frame rate | Not measured: development so far has been on a software renderer without a graphics card |
| Site to mesh, built and natural | Working for rectangular rooms, courts and caves; the span grid itself is not built yet |
| Maps | Plan, ink and the automap are working, each drawn from the site document, and the rendered style is taken from the 3D scene. The illustrated style, layers and vector output are not started. |

Also in place: eye adaptation measured from the image, and first-person walking with collision.

Known weaknesses, in rough order of importance:

- The stone reads as clean and new. It needs stronger weathering, more variation between stones, and broken or missing pieces.
- Moss is a flat overlay. It should have its own relief and gather more convincingly in joints.
- Nothing is in the rooms: no doors, furniture, debris or plants.
- Torch flames are simple animated cards and there are no particles in the air.
- Bake time grows with the size of the place. It is seven times faster than it was, but a large site on a tablet is still a wait.

## What must be proven early

Before much generation is built on top, a hand-assembled test scene has to show the target look is reachable in a browser. Two scenes: a torchlit vaulted hall, and a courtyard under a low sun with a doorway into shadow.

1. A procedural material with convincing normal, roughness and height, blended by a field.
2. Baked indirect light that makes the hall and the arcade read as lit by bounce, computed in the browser in acceptable time.
3. Sun shadows outdoors and a few shadowed local lights indoors at the target frame rate.
4. Fog with visible shafts of light.
5. Span grid to mesh for both built and natural geometry, with no visible grid.
6. The same scenes drawn as a plan map and a rendered map.
