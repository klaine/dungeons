# 3. The tool and the plan

## What we are building

A browser tool that generates places for adventure — delves, strongholds, settlements, wild places and composites of them — and presents each as a 3D world to walk through and as 2D maps.

**The workbench** is for making sites and understanding why they came out as they did.

- Seed and brief controls; presets; a shareable URL per site
- A view per pipeline layer: premise card, mission graph, space graph, plan map
- Map overlays: zones and biomes, critical path, locks and keys, intensity, fields, sightlines
- Re-roll a single layer while holding the others fixed
- A metrics panel, and a gallery of candidates to compare

**The explorer** is for being there.

- First-person 3D in the browser (WebGPU, or WebGL 2 where there is none) with physically based materials, baked and real-time lighting, fog and weather
- Working doors, keys, mechanisms and state changes
- A fly-over camera for seeing the whole site
- An automap that fills in as the player explores

**The assets** are what leaves the tool.

- 2D maps in several styles, per storey, with layers and a numbered key
- The 3D scene as glTF with its materials
- The site document as JSON, carrying the structure a game needs: places, ways, locks, roles

The workbench matters as much as the explorer. Generators are tuned by looking at many outputs and at every intermediate layer; without that visibility quality stalls.

## Scope

In scope for the first version: a single site with a walkable extent up to a few hundred metres across, 15 to 60 places, one to four storeys and two to four biomes; exploration and puzzle gating.

Out of scope for now: combat systems, character progression, multiplayer, a continuous overworld joining sites, authored narrative text, mobile browsers. Threats and inhabitants exist in the data model as abstract placeholders with an intensity value, so a game can interpret them later.

## Technology

| Concern | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Language | TypeScript, strict | Data-model heavy; types are the schema |
| Build | Vite | Fast iteration, static output |
| Hosting | Static site, no backend | Everything runs in the browser; a seed is the save file |
| Generator core | Pure TypeScript, no DOM | Runs in a web worker, and in Node for tests and batch analysis |
| Randomness | Seeded generator, one stream per layer | Determinism, independent re-rolls |
| 3D | three.js with its WebGPU renderer, which falls back to WebGL 2; materials and post-processing written as node materials in three's shading language | Mature, large ecosystem, glTF export, full control of the shading, and one set of shading code for both graphics interfaces |
| Light baking | Ray tracing against the generated geometry in web workers, stored per vertex | The world is static after generation; dense geometry makes vertices a fine enough carrier |
| Textures | Procedural material recipes rendered on the GPU; scanned sets accepted | Seedable, variable, nothing to license |
| 2D maps | Canvas and SVG | Raster and vector output from the same code |
| Tests | Vitest, with property tests across many seeds | Invariants such as "always completable" need thousands of cases |

The renderer and baking choices are being proven by the look milestone (M1) before anything depends on them.

Proposed layout, one package with enforced boundaries:

```
src/
  core/        generator; no DOM, no rendering imports
    rng/  premise/  setting/  mission/  topology/  layout/  geometry/
    history/  population/  dressing/  lighting/  critique/  model/
  content/     data: biomes, site grammars, archetypes, cycle patterns, blueprints, materials
  render/      meshing builders, material shaders, light baking, scene assembly
  maps/        map styles
  export/      json, gltf, images
  app/         workbench and explorer UI
tools/         batch runs, expressive range analysis
docs/
```

## Where things stand

- **Generated sites.** A seed produces a walled house of five to eleven rooms with a forecourt in front and a sanctum at the far end. The plan is one of several arrangements of loops: a ring, a ring round a cloister court, two rings side by side, two rings one behind the other, or rooms off a central gallery joined along the flanks. Any of them may leave one room on a loop open to the sky as a court to cross. Side chambers hang off the loops, some two deep. Rooms vary from narrow passages to halls, floors step up and down through the doorways, and roofs are stone vaults or timber. Structure is built from cycles from the start, so most sites have two or more loops. Every place is still a rectangle.
- **Rooms with a use.** Larger rooms are given a purpose — refectory, dormitory, chapter house, kitchen, store, workshop, scriptorium — and furnished for it from closed blocks of planking: tables and benches, rows of beds, barrels, crates, shelves, desks. A piece is set down only where it is clear of doorways and of everything already placed, and only if every doorway in the room can still be reached from every other; that last check is what keeps furniture from walling off a route.
- **Condition.** A house is kept, damp or forsaken. Damp raises the wet line on the walls. A forsaken house has lost about two fifths of its furniture and one of its roofs: that room lies open to the sky, overgrown, with the fallen blocks still on the floor.
- **Setting.** Each seed fixes what the house is built of (limestone, sandstone, greystone or basalt), the time of day (morning, midday, evening, sunset, dusk or night, which sets the sun's or the moon's height, direction and colour and the sky's), how much cloud there is, and what it is called. The stone tints all masonry and the rock of any cave.
- **Best of several.** Six sites are drafted for each seed and the one with the richest structure is kept, scored on loops, how much longer the true route is than the direct one, keys, a barred door, a court, a cave, a sensible number of dark rooms and variety of roofs. After selection nearly every site has two or more loops and most have two keys.
- **Caves.** About one site in three has a natural cave the builders broke into and walled off, with a door set in a patch of masonry. Most run on beyond the first chamber: two to five chambers of rock, sometimes forking, joined by clefts whose floors slope from one level to the next. The whole cave is carved as one body of air in one body of rock, from a single density function, so there are no joins. The first chamber is as dark as it was found. Beyond it light comes and goes: one chamber may have a hole in its roof where the hill fell in, open to the sky, with ferns at its foot and the sun on its wall; others have growths on the rock that glow, whose light is baked in. Dark and lit chambers alternate, so there is always light ahead. A cave goes down: each chamber beyond the first lies a man's height or more below the one it is reached from, and the cleft between them is a climb, its floor cut down into the floor of the higher chamber so that there is no ledge at either end. Keys are hidden at the far end.
- **The mission.** Each site is given something to overcome. The sanctum is locked and wants a gold key, which is hidden somewhere that takes finding, by preference a dead end and sometimes a dark one. About half the time that room is itself locked with an iron lock, with the iron key elsewhere. About a third of the time one way out of the entrance hall cannot be passed at first, and becomes a shortcut once the player has gone round. It is either a door barred on its far side, or a portcullis whose lever stands just beyond it: the player can see through the bars into the room they cannot yet enter. The lever is the first thing in the game that changes somewhere else when used. Other doorways are hung with doors standing open or shut, or left as plain arches.
- **The valley.** The player arrives at the far end of a valley some hundred and twenty metres across and eighty long, on a worn track that wanders up through woods to a meadow and the door of the house, which is built into the escarpment at the valley's head. All of it can be walked: the ground and the trunks of trees are solid, boulders lie about, and cliffs too high to climb shut the valley in on three sides, with wooded hills and three ranges of mountains beyond them, one behind another to three kilometres, each paler in the haze than the one in front. The meadow before the door and the scarp beside it are carved and baked with the house; the rest of the rim is carved from the same lattice of heights, and the floor of the valley is a sheet laid over the same height function, so all of them meet. What grows is drawn as cards: grass in tufts, thick in the meadow and thin by the track, with flowers in drifts; bushes by rocks; trees with crowns of several hundred leaf cards shaded as one rounded body; firs in drooping tiers on the higher ground; and here and there a stand in blossom. Getting on for three hundred thousand cards in all, moving in the wind. The hill above the house is its roof: mounded turf with rough grass and heather, a coping on the front wall and a stone kerb round each court and roofless room. A roaming test drives the player at the rim and the cliffs from thirty-six directions, jumping, and confirms they neither get out nor fall through.
- **A castle's valley** (20 November 2026, asked for). Every valley is now a castle with a small village below it, and from where the player arrives at its foot up to its head: forest, then fields and a pasture with sheep, then the village, then the castle. The valley is wider (out to where the land has risen seven metres over its stream, not four) and longer (some two hundred metres from the house front). The house always stands free, as the castle's hall and keep, inside a curtain wall round a bailey, with the gate in the front wall between two towers, where the track goes out, and none, one or two towers more at the back corners; its first great room is a chapel and it always has a dungeon under it. The old tower standing alone in the valley is gone (its builder raises the castle's towers), and so are the village's keep on a promontory, the delve, the house built into the ridge, the town, hamlet and manor (`?settle=` still asks for any of them), the terraces, the grottoes and the falls, each of which raised a face of rock on the valley's side. Then the castle was set on a hill with its wall along the brow, towers of several kinds and a tall keep at the back, the road climbing to its gate and the village grown along that road; built of plain solids in a coursed stone of its own, as the house's walls now are too. Then the house was given a castle's look (a parapet and merlons over its outer walls, bartizans at its corners, a taller keep) and the village a square with a well, lanes, houses of several sizes and plots behind them. Then the castle was moved onto the land's own high ground beside the valley, at the brow over its head where the land has a seat for it, with the village up the slope under it along the road down to the stream, and the valley reckoned from there (asked on 20 November 2026). Then the castle was made larger (walls ten to twelve and a half metres, a broader bailey, taller towers and a keep of eight or nine storeys) and its village kept twenty metres off its wall, and a valley's build was brought back from 109 seconds to 58. Then the village's houses were set into the slope, each at the height of the road before its door with the land coming to its walls, and the castle's hill wooded, with trees in the village's gardens, by its square and beside its houses. Then its houses on the hillside were given half basements, with a back door onto a yard at their first storey, and cellars open behind where the land falls away. Then the village grew: twice the houses, a street across the road, a small church of stone in a walled churchyard, and sheds in the yards. Then the castle (valley's and island's alike) was made less artificial: stone of courses and stones of many sizes and tones, hammer-dressed, weathered with moss, damp, splash and streaks, put-log holes, a bailey of trodden earth with lean-to ranges, a well and a stair up to the wall-walk, and a bell-cote on its chapel. Then its walls were made not to repeat: a picture of four metres, laid in patches read from different places in it and joined round the stones, with patches of tone over it and fewer put-log holes; and then the castle bent, its walls bellying and leaning a little, the line of its merlons running up and down. Still to come: low retaining walls where a house's ground stands over the road, the dungeon furnished as one. The island's house is now such a castle too, in place of the castle on a headland it had (20 November 2026).
- **The island.** With `?place=island` the same house stands instead on the high ground of an island in the sea: sea cliffs and stacks all round, a cove with a beach and a jetty where the player arrives, a hamlet of fishermen's cottages and their boats, a lighthouse that can be climbed, a cave in the cove's cliff, and the mainland across the water. Since 20 November 2026 the house is a castle's, within its curtain wall, towers, gate and keep, as a valley's is, and the castle that often stood on a headland is gone. See item 20 below.
- **The town.** With `?place=town` a cathedral stands on a terrace at the head of the market square of a harbour town (item 53: a nave between aisles, transepts, a choir and a shrine, west towers that are climbed to their bells and the walk round their spires, a crypt and a dungeon under it, a cloister beside it, made between one and a half and three times as high as the buildings about the square), on a coast between the sea and the mountains: a harbour with a quay, two moles and a light, where the player arrives; streets of classical buildings by the square and the waterfront, timber-framed and wooden houses beyond, shacks on the fringes and red storehouses along the shore; fields, woods, and an outlook on the headland. See item 37 below.
- **The mountain top.** With `?place=mountain` the same house is a hospice in a saddle on a mountain top that stands out of a sea of cloud, summer or winter. See item 21 below.
- **A secret.** About three sites in five have a chamber that no door leads to. One stretch of wall is really a door faced in stone: its courses do not quite line up with the wall's, and a thin arched outline shows if you look. It holds at two paces and gives when all but leaned on. Inside is a relic. Nothing the mission needs is ever put behind it, the map leaves the chamber off until it is found, and the end of a run says whether you found it.
- **Storeys.** About half the houses have a room over a room, or a cellar under one, or both: a solar, loft or muniment room above, a cellar, undercroft, crypt or cold store below. Each has the outline of the room it belongs to and is reached from it by a stone stair of eighteen steps that runs up one long wall and comes through a railed well in the floor above. Nothing else opens off such a room, so it is a place to go to and come back from; the mission knows about them and hides keys there. The hill over the house is as much higher as the rooms under it need. The plan in play shows the storey the player is on; exported maps show the ground storey, with stairs drawn and what is kept on other storeys marked at them.
- **A tower.** About one valley in four has a tower standing by itself among the trees, eight to fifteen metres off the track with its door towards it: eight metres square outside, walls most of a metre thick, and anything from three storeys to eight, so that its flagged roof behind a parapet with merlons is nine metres up in one valley and twenty-five in another, well over the trees. It stands on a plinth that is battered out at the foot; a band of dressed stone marks each floor; the parapet is carried out over the wall on a row of corbels; a porch of oak posts and boards covers the door, up two steps; the top chamber has windows where the storeys under it have slits; and a banner flies from a pole on one corner. Inside, a stone stair climbs against the north wall, the next against the south, and so on to the roof, each coming up through a railed well; the upper floors are boarded on beams, each storey has a torch, and somebody left a hunting horn on the roof. From the roof the valley is seen over the trees. It is not part of the house or of its mission.
- **Buildings by the track.** About a third of the valleys have buildings in them, facing the track, on ground levelled for them. In a quarter it is a hamlet of one to three timber-framed houses, as in the old towns of Alsace and the Moselle: a plastered ground storey on a stone plinth with a boarded door, shuttered windows and a bench; over it one or two storeys of oak framing (sill and head beams, posts, a rail under the windows, braces, crosses under the sills), each jettied out over the street on its joist ends, the spaces between washed cream, ochre, rose, blue, sage or red; boxes of flowers on the sills; a steep roof of clay tiles with a framed gable to the street or its eaves and dormers; a stone chimney. No two are alike, and the first of a row is the inn and hangs out a sign. In a seventh it is one stuccoed classical building of the early nineteenth century, anything from a pavilion of one storey and three windows to a block of five storeys or thirteen windows (the broad ones are rare, for want of room in a valley): a granite plinth, a rusticated ground storey, a moulded band, tall windows in moulded surrounds with cornices on brackets and a pediment in every other bay, pilaster strips, a deep cornice on blocks, a low hipped roof of sheet metal laid in seams, plastered chimneys, and usually a centre of three bays standing forward under four pilasters and a pediment, with the door up three steps in the middle of it. Walls are washed yellow, rose, green, blue-grey or cream and everything moulded is white. Every timber, moulding, glazing bar and pane is a solid of its own. Storeys are not all one height: each building has its own, a tall ground storey and lower rooms above in the timber houses, a taller principal storey in the classical ones, and windows are as tall as their storey allows. The classical building can be gone into, and is planned inside as such houses were, by rule from its bays; the plan is part of the site document, and is drawn on the map at the level of the ground and as a sheet of floor plans, one to a storey. Its outer walls are three quarters of a metre thick, the wall down its middle half a metre, and the walls between rooms thirty centimetres, so windows sit in deep reveals with a board for a sill inside and a deep stone or moulded sill outside; the timber houses' stone ground storeys are more than half a metre thick. About seven in ten of those seven bays broad or more, where there is room behind them, have wings reaching back from their ends about a court, so that the building is an L or a U; a wing has the main block's storeys, a row of rooms one opening into the next down its length, and a deep door into the main block's end rooms through what was its back wall, and its own cornice and hipped roof, which runs in under the main block's. About two in five of the taller ones are set into rising ground: the ground climbs along their sides and stands at the height of the ground floor behind them and on back towards the valley's side, so the lowest storey is open to the front and buried at the back, without windows where the ground stands against it, and there is a second door at the back onto the higher ground. Its middle is the way in and the way up: an entrance hall paved in squares of two stones, and behind it a hall with a stone stair of two flights and a landing, railed, which goes on up to every storey; or, where the middle is one bay, a passage through the house with the stair at its far end. Either side is a wing of rooms in two rows, each room one to three bays broad and the two wings alike, with doors in a line from room to room along the front and a door from each front room to the room behind. Every storey is planned the same. Rooms are each painted their own colour, with a skirting, a rail and a cornice, boarded floors, and in the front rooms a stove of glazed tiles; there is a table and benches in the room over the entrance. The sun comes in at the windows and lies on the floors. The timber houses can be gone into as well, and are planned storey by storey in the site document: the door, to one side of the front, opens into a hall along that side of the house with the stair in it against the side wall; a framed wall divides it from the room, which has the windows to the street, a table and benches, and a stove of green tiles against the hall's wall; upstairs the stair comes up into a landing with two chambers off it, one to the street and one to the back, each with a bed and a chest. Floors are boards on joists that are seen from below, and are the same joists whose ends carry each storey out over the street. The stair is steep, as such stairs are: straight flights one over another, the lowest boxed in, those above open treads between two strings, with a screen of slats along the open side that also keeps anyone on a landing out of the well. The player steps up anything to about a third of a metre. (That was first written here two commits before it was true: the stride tried from a step higher was too short to bring the round foot over the step's edge, and the body was let down onto the step all at once and so pushed back out of its face, so a step of twenty centimetres still stopped it. Found when the timber houses' doorsteps did; both are mended, and a body now walks every stair of three timber houses and of a classical building, and the roaming test on three valleys still finds no way out of them.)
- **A town of brick.** About one valley in five has a little town instead of a village (half of those meant to have a village; `?settle=town` asks for one): a street of houses along the track where the valley is broadest, their fronts to it on one line, wall against wall, with a lane between now and then, in the manner of the towns of the Baltic coast (Lübeck along the Trave above all). Most are merchants' houses with their steep gables to the street: stepped, or between piers that rise above the gable into pinnacles, or in curved tiers with scrolls, or plain; a tall ground storey for the hall with a broad door in a portal, storeys of windows above, often standing in tall pointed niches plastered white, the gable with its own rows of niches and windows to the lofts, and at its top a hatch under a hoist beam with its wheel and rope. Some are broader houses of a later century with their eaves to the street, rendered and washed in pale colours with white surrounds, a cornice and a small gable of their own over the middle bays; some are warehouses with a column of loading doors and a timber housing for the winch at the gable's top. Fronts are bare brick or rendered; there are bands of dark glazed brick, a sawtooth frieze at the gable's foot, iron anchors at the floors. At one end of the street stands a church of the brick Gothic: a hall church, its walls buttressed bay by bay with tall windows of two or three lights in tracery, a frieze of little pointed arches under its eaves, a portal of stepped orders; a choir with an apse or a flat gabled end, under the nave's own stepped or pinnacled gable; and a tower, or two, in stages with white niches, a belfry with louvres and a clock, under a needle spire of green copper among four gables, a pyramid with corner spirelets, or a ridged roof between stepped gables. The houses can be entered (see *Inside the houses of brick and the canal houses*, below); the church cannot.
- **Canal houses.** A style of its own (`canal`), as along the canals of Amsterdam: fronts from one window broad to seven, three to five storeys (a warehouse's four to six), deep lots, under any of six outlines over the eaves: a spout gable with its slopes boarded and tarred, a stepped gable capped in stone, a neck gable with claw pieces (one tier or two), a bell gable edged in stone, a straight cornice on brackets, or such a cornice with a panel raised over its middle; crowned with a pediment, a segmental pediment, a scrolled crest with a vase, or a ball. They are dressed in four degrees, from plain brick with flat arches over the windows to stone bands, keystones, crowned windows, garlands, pilasters storey over storey and a balustrade; fronts are bare brick or painted; windows are sashes of small panes or of one, or cross windows with shutters under carved relieving arches; a stoop climbs to a raised ground storey over a basement, with iron railings, or a painted timber front fills the ground storey; warehouses have a column of loading doors painted in two colours. Every front leans out over the street by its own amount, and carries a hoist beam at its top. They line the river's quay in a harbour town, and in two towns in five (Dutch ones) the waterfront and every side of the classical quarter's blocks but the square's. They can be entered.
- **Inside the houses of brick and the canal houses.** Each house is planned as its kind was: a merchant's house its tall hall flagged for carts, with the office partitioned off at the front, its first floor lived in and lofts above; a warehouse open floors one over another; a broad house or a canal double house a passage through the middle with rooms either side; the common canal house a corridor along one side with the stair in it and a front and a back room beside; the narrowest its rooms one behind another. Walls between rooms with doorways cased in oak, floors of boards on beams seen from below, whitewashed walls, and a steep stair of straight flights one over another up to the top storey; front doors stand open. Nothing is furnished. The plans are in the site document, are drawn on the maps and as sheets of floor plans, and a body walks in from the street, up the stoop, and up every flight. Windows of every building that can be entered (these, the timber and the classical) are clear glass, seen through both ways.
- **High ground and caves in the valley.** About two valleys in five have a terrace: a shelf of level ground against one side wall, three and a half to five metres up, with a cliff of its own below it and the rest of the wall behind. The way up is a ledge rising along the wall at about one in three, or steps cut in the rock at one in two. At its far end somebody has built a cairn, and left a spyglass; from there the valley is seen whole. About half the valleys have a grotto: a cave in a side wall, entered at its foot, whose passage bends in under the hill to a small chamber where growths glow on the rock and a lamp was left. Two in five of those are not caves at all but a hall hewn under the hill: a dressed doorway in the rock face, a broad stair of thirty-odd steps going down, and at its foot a hall twenty to thirty metres long and nine to eleven high, with two rows of square pillars, torches on them, a roof that rises to a crown, and at the far end a dais with a tomb and a crown of gold on it. And about half of those halls are great ones, some fifty-five metres long, twenty-five across and twenty-two high. The stair does not come down to the floor of such a hall but out onto a ledge across its near end, twelve metres up, so the hall is first seen whole and from above: pillars the full height of it, a shaft of daylight coming down through the roof onto the floor, and a chasm across the floor with a bridge over it. A stair goes down from the ledge along one wall. The other wall is a screen of arched openings in two tiers, with a gallery behind the upper tier and an aisle behind the lower. From the gallery a second bridge crosses the hall high over the chasm to a door in the far wall and a small room behind it. The chasm is ten metres deep, can be fallen into without harm, and has steps cut up its far side. There are three things to find: on the tomb, in the room over the bridge, and at the bottom of the chasm. A hall of either kind has light of its own: its torches, and in a great hall the sun or moon down the shaft and what comes of it; none of the valley's daylight reaches in. Nobody gets out of the valley from any of them.
- **Rock.** The wall round the valley and the scarp at its head are cliffs, not slopes: beds of hard and soft stone, worn back by a simulation of frost and gravity into faces and ledges, then carved as a solid with the beds stepped, split into blocks and broken into buttresses, and scree at the foot. Each of the four stones makes a different cliff. Boulders are blocks with flat faces, cracks and beds; standing stones are tapered slabs with broken tops, fluted by rain. Rock has a material of its own, laid on from three sides so that it never stretches. The mountains beyond have ridges and gullies, forest, pasture and snow. How it is done is in document 4.
- **Flying.** `V` lifts the player off the ground: no weight, nothing solid, moving the way they look. It is for seeing a place whole and for taking pictures; a camera placed by the address (`?cam=`) flies too, so it stays where it is put.
- **Trees and what grows with them.** Broadleaved trees are grown: a trunk forks into limbs, branches and twigs, and the leaves hang in sprays on the twigs, dark inside the crown and bright at its edge. There is a spreading kind and a tall kind, old trees among the young, roots at their feet, bushes of many stems, fallen trunks and stumps. Conifers are grown as well, spruce and pine, with whorls of real branches. The pond stands in beds of reed, the moist ground is knee-deep in meadow grass, and ivy climbs the front of the house, more of it the longer the house has been left.
- **Water.** Most valleys have water, and no two alike: a pool a few paces across, a pond, or a lake that fills its side of the valley, each with an irregular shore, rocks in its shallows and sometimes an island with a tree. Still water is a mirror: the trees, the cliffs, the mountains and the clouds stand in it, and its shallow edge is clear. There is also running water: a brook or a small river rising at a spring among boulders under the rim, running into the pond and out of it again to a sink; or a stream alone. The ground is shaped to carry it, with beds cut so that it always falls. Running water is the same water as the still: it mirrors what stands over it, its wet bed of cobbles shows through it, its ripples travel down the current, it breaks white where it falls steeply, and where it runs into a pond or out of one the two are one surface. Two streams in five come into the valley over the rim as a waterfall, down a face worn smooth for them, with spray at the foot. Most water keeps to one side of the track; in about one valley in six it crosses, by stepping stones if it is a brook and by a timber bridge if it is a river. Water can be heard: a brook as a quiet rush with small notes over it, a fall as a roar that opens up as you come near, and a splash for each step taken in a stream.
- **Things in the valley.** The site document lays out the valley: how wide and how long it is, the track, and two or three things set back from the track far enough to be a walk and near enough to be noticed: a pond, a ring of standing stones, the ruin of a stone hut. The season is part of the setting, and colours everything that grows: blossom and fresh green in spring, deeper green and drier grass in summer, gold, rust and straw in autumn. The pond lies in a hollow levelled for it, with still water that mirrors the sky under drifting ripples, reeds at its edge, and a landing stage of planks on posts built out from the bank nearest the track. The stones are tall rough slabs on a low rise, one of them fallen, round a flat stone. Something small is left at each for whoever walks that far, and the end of a run says how many were picked up. A post-and-rail fence runs beside the track where it enters the valley.
- **Sound.** Made in the browser from noise and a few oscillators, with no recordings: wind that rises and falls and leaves out of doors, a low room tone under a roof, footsteps that follow the stride and tell grass from earth, stone, planks and water, running water and falls near enough to hear, and small sounds for doors, locks, keys, the lever, finds and the end. It can be turned off, and that is remembered. It has been measured but never heard: only a headless browser was available, so levels are set from numbers and the character of each sound is untested.
- **Proof of completion.** A solver searches over where the player is, which keys they hold, which bars they have lifted and which levers they have thrown, and every generated site must have a solution or it is rejected. Nothing the player does can be undone, so a site that can be completed from the start can be completed from anywhere.
- **Play.** Everything is done by walking: coming up to a door tries it, walking up to a key takes it, reaching the chest in the sanctum ends the run. Lock plates are the colour of their key.
- **Checks.** A batch tool (`npx tsx tools/sites.ts`) generates hundreds of sites and confirms each is connected, can be completed, and has no doorway too steep for its stairs. In the browser, a walk test moves the player's body through every door and arch in both directions, and a playthrough test follows the solver's route with the player's body, letting doors and keys respond as in play.
- **Export.** From the Export menu: the site document as JSON (with the solver's route), the plan and the ink map as PNGs with a numbered key, and the 3D model as a binary glTF with its textures and stone tints. Meshes are named for their place and doors for their way, so the document can be used to find parts of the model. The model reloads through the standard glTF loader with every mesh intact; a ten-room site is about 50 MB. Baked light is not in the model. The format is described in [document 5](05-site-format.md).
- **Ink map.** The same site drawn as a referee's map: bare paper for floor inside a heavy, slightly unsteady line, hatching in the solid ground that fades out away from the walls, ruled flagstones, stippled courts and caves, rubble where a roof fell, and signs for locked and barred doors, the portcullis and its lever, the secret door, and keys. It is drawn from the document and the seed alone.
- **Battle map.** A picture of the built world from straight above, for tabletop play. It is taken twice and put together by place: roofed rooms with everything above head height cut away and a weak even light added so unlit rooms can be read, courts and open ground whole and in daylight. Cut walls come out black. It is ruled in 1.5 metre squares with the image's edges on grid lines, and the hidden chamber is blacked out. Indoors it is evenly lit and rather flat: the torches' own light is left out, because only four are ever lit at once.
- **Example viewer.** `examples/viewer/` reads an exported model and document without using any of the tool's code: it cuts the building open at a chosen height, names the rooms, and draws the solver's route through them.
- **Gallery.** `gallery.html` generates places a dozen at a time and shows each as a plan with its description, loop count and route length. It can be asked for places with a court, a cave, a portcullis, a hidden chamber or a fallen roof, or for small or large ones. A dozen take about a fifth of a second, because only the document is generated; choosing one opens it in the explorer.
- **Building lab.** `lab/buildings.html` is a square apart from the game where any kind of building the game builds can be added, moved, turned, scaled and reshaped by its measures or by dragging arrows on its faces, built by the game's own builders and drawn with its materials; what is set out can be undone, is kept in the browser and can be saved as site documents. See item 25 below and the README.
- **Figure lab.** `lab/figures.html` is a stage apart from the game where figures are steered from behind or sent about by pointing: they walk, run, sprint, creep, jump, climb ledges, sit, lie down and get up, and play any of 85 clips. They are also in the building lab, added as buildings are and given orders from a palette, finding their way through doors and up stairs. They are not yet in the game or the world. See items 55 and 56 below and the README.
- **Range.** `npm run range` generates 2,000 sites and charts what came out: loops against route length, how often each feature appears, plan shapes, and rooms and unlit rooms per site. Each seed has a size to aim for, so houses run from five rooms to eleven.
- **Map.** A plan of the site is drawn from the same document and shown as a minimap. Out of doors it takes in the whole valley, with the track, the pond and the stones; indoors it shows the house alone. It fills in as the player explores: places entered are drawn in full, places seen through an open doorway are sketched, the rest is left off. Shut doors and keys seen but not taken are marked.
- **Light and dark.** Each place is lit by day, by torches, or deliberately left dark; the player carries a small light for the dark ones. The plan map shades unlit rooms.
- **Lighting many rooms.** Four shadow-casting lights are shared among all torches and follow the player; rooms further off are lit by baked bounce light only until the player comes near.
- **The hand-built scene** is kept as a fixed test bed for the look, at `?scene=test`.

## Left to do: rock, grass and trees (17 November 2026)

A session borrowed ideas from two outside projects (Abyssal Garden for rock and plants, `teadrinker/foliage-shader` for light through crowns), tried them in a standalone page (`lab/rock-lab.ts`, untracked), and ported what the game did not already have. The game turned out to have most of it already (triplanar rock with beds and streaks, stones that break in flat faces, crown-shaded leaf cards with backlight, culling of grass by blocks). What was added is below with what is still open.

**Done and committed:** flat-face shading on sharp stone (`uRockFacet`); leaf and needle cards cast only their denser middle into the sun's shadow (`?leafcut`, 0.7); grass as thirty real blades within 15 m, painted tufts beyond (`?blades=0` to turn off); shade variation and gaps in broadleaf crowns; trunk bend, fluting and oval sections; trees of every age (giants, young, a rare spreading oak); richer bark texture.

**Not done, and not checked:**

- *Nothing was measured on a real graphics card.* The embedded browser pane's frame times swung from 7 to 30 ms for the same view, so only triangle counts are reliable: the blades added about 18% (2.55M to 3.02M on one seed). Frame cost of the blades, the crown changes and the shadow cutoff is unknown, and an iPad has still not been tried.
- *Facet shading* was looked at on one greystone and one basalt seed only. Limestone, sandstone, boulders and cave roofs are untried, and the strength (a fifth of the flat normal, 0.7 of edge light) was set by eye on one cliff. Pushed harder, the carved mesh's own triangles show and the grain of the texture is flattened.
- *Distance detail for rock chunks and boulders.* In the lab, tiers for rubble took the wide view from 11M to 0.66M triangles, mostly in the rubble. The game's rock and boulders have not been profiled for the same thing. Grass and leaves are already cut by blocks; rock is not.
- *Leaf shadow cutoff* was compared on one view, in two separate loads. It also lets more sun through pines. Check other hours (low sun, dusk), other seeds, and that nothing looks too bright where crowns are dense.
- *Grass blades:* check, walking through a meadow, that blades give way to tufts at 15 m without a visible ring or a change of colour or density. Painted tufts are not shrunk over the hand-over (that sent far tufts into the sky); a block straddling 15 m draws both, so there is a band of denser grass there. Blocks for grass are 12 m. Could try fewer, wider blades at mid range, or a third tier.
- *Crown colour and gaps* were seen on one seed. Autumn palettes (the warm shift may stack with the season's colours), blossom trees and far trees are unchecked. Conifers are untouched: their needle sprays use a different path, and a pine or spruce has none of the shade variation or gaps.
- *Trunks and bark:* oak and ash share one bark texture and differ only by vertex tint; a separate ash bark needs a second material (the mesher, `main.ts` and the export's texture list all know the material names). The new lichens, flecks and warm and cool patches were never seen lit and close; trunks were in shade in every picture. Trunk shape applies only above detail 0.4; far trees and whole-tree tufts show no bend, flutes or age.
- *Age classes:* about one tree in twenty is a giant, three in ten young, and rarely an oak of great age that spreads (`wide`). The spreading oak was seen only forced and from inside: its limbs ran long and straight with leaf thin at the tips (more bend and more cards were added after that, unseen). Nothing yet keeps the ground under it clear or shades the plants beneath, and `canopies` (which place understory) take its reach but not its roof. Giants and young trees do not change what grows below them either.
- *Looking at trees:* `?agemix` (every age class in turn) and `?treerow=x,z` (a row of broadleaf kinds alone on the meadow) are in `siteMesher.ts` but uncommitted and not in the README. The row ran into a cliff at its end on the one seed tried, so the spreading oak and the giants were never photographed standing clear. A row of conifers is not there.
- *The material lab* (`lab/materials.ts`, `lab/specimens.ts`, `npm run lab`) was built after this list, for taking rock, ground and leaves further: unlike the page below it draws the game's own materials on the game's own geometry, and takes up a saved material file where it stands. It has a land brush, for painting new masses of rock onto the stage to see the material on shapes of one's own (`carveMass` in `specimens.ts`), which also paints plants: single kinds of tree, a mixed wood, bushes, grass, flowers and ferns (`growPainted`). The camera flies on W A S D. What it does not have: water, masonry and buildings, the far-land material, a baked light (everything on it is lit as the valley's unbaked scenery is, by the sun and the whole sky), clouds and their shadows, and a way to see one channel of a material alone (its colour without light, its normal, its roughness). The makers are called in `forge` as `main.ts` calls them, by hand: if what `main.ts` gives a maker changes, change it there too.
- *Limestone* was the first stone taken through the lab (see "Limestone, and what carving can hold" in `04-look.md`). Left: the shader's thin beds do not lie where the carved beds do (the material does not know the strata; a point's place among the beds could be carried in the third field, which rock does not use); the open planes still run a little long and level seen from thirty metres; close up the surface wants the pitting and sharp little flutes of real karst, which is a matter for the `rock` texture recipe or a second one for worn stone; nothing grows on the ledges; boulders of limestone are not yet rounded or grooved as the cliff is; and the valley's own land does not make towers, which were made here by the lab's cliff alone. Sandstone, greystone and basalt have `uneven` and `worn` at 0 and are next.
- *The old lab* (`lab/rock-lab.ts`) is standalone WebGL and GLSL, not three's shading language, and nothing imports it. Keep it as a record of the ideas or delete it. Ideas from it not taken: four-scale triplanar rock with bed bands (the game's two-scale version is richer in beds, poorer in scale), moss by normal and height, the cell-and-tier scheme for rubble.

## Direction received, and where it goes

Three things were asked for after the valley became walkable.

1. **Shrubs and other plants besides grass and trees; how to place them; how to decide where grass is taller.** Done as a first pass: ferns, tall herbs, heather, thicket, saplings and nettles, placed by four numbers computed for every point of the valley (shade, wet, wear, thin soil), which also set the height and colour of the grass. The rules are in document 4.
2. **Cave sections much longer and more complex than one room, with some light at times.** Done as a first pass: caves of up to five chambers joined by sloping clefts, with a daylight hole or glowing growths in alternate chambers. Since then chambers lie on different levels, one to two metres apart, with clefts that climb between them; and there are caves in the valley's own walls, which have nothing to do with the house. Still to do: caves that are not confined to the house's grid of cells, water underground, and a way out to the valley from the house's own cave, which would be a second way into the house and so has to be told to the solver.
3. **Verticality of spaces, built and natural.** Natural first, as agreed, and that part is done as a first pass: terraces in the valley reached by a climb, caves on more than one level, and grottoes in the valley's walls. (A note came while it was being built: man-made ways cut in the rock, stairways and the like, where they suit the place. Some terraces are now reached by cut steps.) Still to do on the natural side: only one terrace and one grotto to a valley, both always against a side wall; no ledges part-way up the higher wall; nothing to climb at the valley's head or foot; and the player neither jumps down with any consequence nor climbs anything steeper than a path. Built verticality has begun: version 2 of the site document lets places lie one above another and a way be a stair, and houses now have cellars and upper rooms. Still to do: rooms on an upper storey that open into one another, so that there is a second way round up there; a gallery over a hall, which is a storey that looks down into the room below; stairs that turn, and stairs in a turret of their own; towers that rise out of the house itself, above the hill; and more buildings that stand free, which is milestone 6 proper. The first of those is built: a tower in the valley, with walls that have two faces, three storeys and a roof. What it does not have yet: any shape but square, any height but three storeys, a roof of its own (it is flat), a door that shuts, anything in its rooms, a place in the mission, or a neighbour. A gatehouse, a hut that is not a ruin, and a group of buildings with a wall round them come next, and they want the site document to say what a building is rather than, as now, that there is a tower at a point.

4. **Rock faces, cliffs and boulders that are not monotonous: noise in layers, thermal erosion, Voronoi fracture, and proper texturing.** Done; see *Rock* above. Two reference pictures were supplied (a canyon of stepped red sandstone with plants on its ledges, and a grey sea crag broken in flat faces); they are not kept in the repository. A first version came out rounded, as if melted; the carving was then changed to one that keeps edges, and ledges, blocks and fracture faces now have true corners. Still missing against the pictures: plants growing on the ledges, and form finer than the 30 centimetre lattice, which only the texture carries.
5. **Trees of higher fidelity, with trunks and branches; plants generally to the level of a third reference picture (an abandoned dwelling among broadleaved trees by reedy water).** Well begun: trees are grown with real branching, old trees, roots, bushes, dead wood, reed beds, meadow grass and ivy on the house. Still to do: different leaves for different kinds of tree, climbers on trunks and on the ruin, plants on the cliff ledges, lilies and weed in the water, and the clutter of things people left about a dwelling.

6. **Atmosphere: clouds with volume that affect the light, and proper sunset, dusk and night.** Done as a first pass: sunset, dusk and night are hours a site can have, each with its own sun or moon, sky, fog and exposure; clouds are a marched layer with volume that takes the hour's light and casts drifting shadows on the land. Still to do: the hour does not change while you are there; clouds do not dim the sky's own light or the baked light, only the direct sun; no rain or wind that rises; the moon has no phases. (Asked since: can cloud ever cover the peaks? It could not, being drawn only where sky showed. It now stands in front of whatever it is nearer than, hangs lower the more of it there is, and the far range goes up into it.)

7. **Water that varies: ponds of very different size and shape, streams and rivers where the land would carry them, lakes and larger water where it would hold them.** Done as a first pass. A pond is anything from a pool to a lake, with a shore that swells where there is room and draws in from the track. Streams rise at springs under the rim, feed the pond and leave it for a sink at the foot of the rim, or run down one side of a valley that has no pond; each is cut a bed that falls all the way, so water never runs uphill. Since done: waterfalls over the rim, fords and bridges where water crosses the track, and the sound of water. Still to do: nothing wider than four metres; a sink is boulders, not a cave mouth; a fall has no pool to speak of at its foot and the rock beside it is not wet; a bridge is always the same bridge. A note followed that the join between mirroring still water and running water was ugly, and that running water should be as good: it now shares the still water's picture, colour and clear shallows, follows its banks, has a bed of wet cobbles, and fades into the pond where they meet. Still to do there: running water casts and takes no shadow, has no wet margin on its banks, and what it mirrors close by is a little out of place, which shows only where it is calm.
8. **A cloudy sunset with depth in the air** (a fourth reference picture: ridge behind ridge fading into blue haze, mist lying in the hollows, a bank of flat cloud with the sun behind it, firs in silhouette). Done as a first pass. Haze thickens with distance and fades land into the sky behind it; mist lies on the valley floor and in the hollows of the far country, by the hour and the season; there are three ranges of hills where there was one ring; cloud can lie in flat banks; and the haze towards a low sun is alight. Still to do: the mist does not move or burn off; cloud does not dim the sky's own light; light does not come down through cloud in shafts; the sun's disc has no softened edge in haze; and nothing of it has been seen on a tablet.
9. **Far landscape that looks better in shape, texture and apparent foliage: "some trickery".** Done as a first pass, and trickery is what it is: far land has a material of its own that works out its cover, the crowns of its woods and its gullies for each point of the picture, and the two nearer ranges carry some eight thousand whole trees painted one to a tuft of cards. The needles the mountains stood in are worn down. Still to do: the far range is too far for any of this and is shape and haze only; nothing casts a shadow out there, so a hill does not shade the hollow behind it; the ring of open ground between the valley's own trees and the foothill woods is bare; there are no crags or outcrops on the hills, only beds of rock where it is steep; and there is nothing made out there, no track, wall, field or smoke.
10. **The track is a smooth orange blur, not gravel or a beaten path; the stones of a stream end abruptly at its banks; ground materials look like placeholders; would displacement and other tricks help, and how is detail to be managed near, in the middle distance and far off?** (With a sixth reference picture: a cart track of two pale gritty ruts with grass in clumps between them and long grass up to its ragged edges.) Done as a first pass: ground has a material of its own that makes turf, beaten earth, shingle and leaf litter and the ragged edges between them for each point of the picture; the track is two ruts with grass down the middle; a stream's stones thin out up its banks over shingle; bare ground near the eye is drawn with depth by parallax. What was decided about displacement and about detail at each distance is in the look document under *Materials*. Still to do: turf with nothing standing on it is still plain, and wants either more grass or a better mat; mud at the water's edge; puddles in the ruts; the flags before the door do not break up into the track; leaf litter does not follow the season; and none of it has been seen on a tablet, where six textures and parallax on the ground are the first thing to measure.
11. **Underground spaces of every kind and size: natural, crafted, or part one and part the other; sometimes modest, sometimes spectacular, monument-like, with the halls of Moria as the picture to have in mind.** Begun at both ends. At the modest and natural end there are caves of a few chambers and grottoes; at the other there is a hewn hall under the hill, in about one valley in five, with a dressed doorway, a stair down and two rows of pillars. A note came after the first of those was seen: it "looks still a bit sad", and now and then such a place must be a place of wonder to explore, with a seventh reference picture (a vast cavern seen from a ledge: shafts of sunlight slanting through dusty air from an opening above, columns many storeys tall, a chasm crossed by bridges at two levels, waterfalls, built fronts and galleries in the walls at several heights, warm light against cool blue depth). So half the halls are now great halls, described under *High ground and caves* above, and every hall has light of its own in place of a grey share of the sky. Of that picture, what is still missing: water falling inside the hall and running in the chasm; more than one shaft of light, and a mouth to the shaft that is ragged and not round; built fronts higher up the walls than the gallery, balconies, stairs that climb the walls in more than one flight; carving on the pillars, the screen and the doorway beyond a plain moulding; the cool blue of the far end, which is only a little there; and any shape of hall but the one. Still to do besides: everything between cave and hall, which is a natural cave that has been worked on (a squared doorway, a levelled floor, steps cut where it climbs); and the record in the site document of how far a space has been worked, which today says only cave, hall or great hall.
12. **The underground as one system, joined to the house.** The note, as given: an enormous adventure hall, grotto and tunnel hybrid system could connect via caves and caverns and constructed or semi-constructed pathways to the main building, a castle for example. Not begun. Today a hall or grotto is a thing by itself in the valley's wall, and the house's caves are rooms of the house. What it wants: halls, caverns and tunnels as places and ways of the site document, on levels below the house, so that the solver and the mission know them; a way down from a cellar or a cave of the house and a way out at a grotto's mouth in the valley, so that the hill can be gone through and not only into; passages that are natural in one stretch and cut in the next. The great hall was laid out with this in mind: the room off its upper bridge and the far ends of its gallery and aisle are where doors onward would go. **Begun (17 November 2026): a way through the hill.** Where a cave chamber of a delve or of a house under the hill lies at its outer edge beside an arm of the valley, a tunnel runs from it straight out through the ridge, six to sixteen metres, to a cave mouth in the ridge's side; about one place in three has one. So the hill can now be gone through: in at the front, out in the arm by the path to the cave or the ruin there, or the other way. It starts only from a chamber that can be reached with nothing in hand, so it is no way round a lock, a bar, a lever or a secret door, and the solver need not know of it. **And a level below (same day): a cavern under the arm.** From three in four of those tunnels (about one place in eight, since it needs room: no hall or grotto near on that side, no room of the house in the way, and not so far south as to come under the meadow) a passage turns off inside the ridge, runs eight metres along it and then out under the arm, falling eight and a half metres on the way, to a cavern twelve to sixteen metres across and five high under the arm's floor, with stalagmites and stalactites, six growths that glow green round its walls, and something left in the middle to find. Walked down and back by the test body on a delve (w4) and a house under the hill (w2); five to six metres of rock lie between its roof and the grass. Not good enough yet: the stalagmites are plain cones and the growths show as white specks. **Joined to the cave in the arm (same day).** Where the tunnel through the hill and a natural cave in the valley's wall are in the same arm (13 seeds in 200), they are now one system. The cavern is put under the hill behind the arm's outer wall; a passage leaves the side of the cave's chamber, follows the foot of that wall under the crag for fourteen to thirty-six metres going down all the way, and runs into the cavern's end; the way from the house comes down inside the ridge, crosses level and deep under the arm, and comes into the cavern's side. So one can go in at the cave in the arm and come out in the house, or the other way. The first try put the cavern under the arm itself and found room in no seed that held up: arms draw in towards their heads. Walked both ways by the test body on w8, w61 and w69, with the tests for getting out, for doorways and for playing to the end; w4, which has a cavern and no cave, walked again. Not good enough yet: where the land's carving hands over to the passage's own, a stride inside the cave's wall, the two overlap raggedly; the passage and the cavern are not on the maps. **Halls joined too (same day).** A hall cut under the hill from the same arm now has a door onward: in a plain hall a squared doorway in a side wall between two pillars, with a torch beside it; in a great hall a door in the far wall of the room over the upper bridge, which was made to have one. A passage goes from it ten to thirty metres to the cavern, which lies beyond the hall to that side. To keep the two ways into the cavern apart (in the first version they could meet one over the other, or pass close under each other), the way from the house now runs along inside the ridge until it is level with the cavern, up to thirty-six metres, and comes into its side, while the other comes into its end; the check for this had a sign wrong in the first version and was passing by luck. With that, 19 seeds in 200 have a joined system: 16 from a cave and 3 from a hall (w3 and w48 plain, w16 great); most halls in arms are refused because the way from the house would have to pass through or under them. 13. **The renderer is to move from WebGL to WebGPU, before the features above are taken further.** Asked for while the great hall was being built, to be done as soon as it was finished. Done. The program is drawn with three.js's WebGPU renderer, which uses WebGL 2 by itself in a browser that has no WebGPU, so nobody is shut out; `?webgl` forces the fallback. Everything that was a standard material with pieces of shader patched into it (masonry, ground, rock, far land, leaves and grass), and everything that was a shader of its own (running and still water, spray, torch flames, the air, mist, cloud, lit dust and shafts) was written again as node materials, once, for both. The light bake needed nothing: it never used the graphics card. Seen side by side with the last WebGL build from the same spots, the two measure the same brightness to three figures and look the same, and frames take no longer (document 4 has the numbers). What changed in passing: baked light is kept in one run of memory per mesh, because WebGPU takes eight runs and there were nine; still water's second drawing of the scene is three's own mirror, at one sample to a point; the battle map is cut open by where the eye is put and not by a clipping plane; a picture's brightness can no longer be read back at once, so the eye's adjustment and the test captures wait a frame for it. What is left: the procedural textures are still painted by a small WebGL renderer at start, from GLSL recipes; grass and leaves read a shade darker and less yellow than the old renderer drew them, on both interfaces (why has not been tracked down, and it is to be left: the darker look was preferred when it was seen); the download is about twice what it was (the node system is large); nothing has been tried on a tablet or a phone on either interface, which is now the first thing to try; and none of what WebGPU makes possible that WebGL did not (compute for the bake, for grass, for the wearing-down of cliffs) is used yet.
14. **The tower is plain: nothing on its outside, no windows to speak of. 15. **Generators for buildings, and their materials, in two styles.** Begun, and both styles stand (see *Buildings by the track* above): the site document describes a building in a few numbers and a generator makes it; plaster, clay tile, sheet metal and glass are new materials. Since then the classical building has been given its range of sizes and an interior (asked for with the remark that buildings of that time often have layouts that can be generated for different sizes: they can, and are). What is still missing: the timber houses cannot be entered; the classical rooms have almost nothing in them but their stoves, and no doors hung in their doorways; the plan of the rooms is worked out by the builder, by rule and from the seed, and is not yet in the site document, so no map shows it and the house's mission knows nothing of it; light indoors is the sky's, dimmed, with the sun added where it comes in, and has none of the warmth light takes from walls and floors; ceilings are dark; they stand only square to the track and only beside it, never in a street, round a square or against one another; the classical building has no columns in the round, no arched windows, no rounded corner and no balcony (asked since, and done: a middle without its pediment; L and U plans with towers of one to three storeys at their corners; a carriage way under an arch into the yard of a closed block; more than one entrance in a broad front; and storeys of other heights than a tall principal storey over a low ground one, a tall ground storey or all alike; see `docs/04-look.md`); the timber houses have no curved braces, no carved posts, no oriel windows, no galleries, no shop fronts; roofs have no gutters or downpipes; glass does not light up at dusk; nothing weathers; and the tower is still built its own way and has not been made a building of this kind. The note as given: "Imperial style": the stuccoed classical town building of the early 1800s, as round the market squares of St Petersburg and Helsinki (long fronts in pale colour, a rusticated ground storey, tall windows in even rows with moulded surrounds, pilasters or columns and a pediment at the middle, a heavy cornice, a low metal roof). And timber-framed building, as in the old town of Colmar and the towns of the Moselle such as Bernkastel-Kues (a stone ground storey, upper storeys of dark posts, rails and braces with pale or coloured infill, each storey jettied out over the one below, steep tiled roofs with dormers, gables to the street, no two houses alike). Not begun. This is milestone 6 given its direction: a building is to be a thing the site document describes (its plan, its storeys, its style, its openings) and a generator makes; the tower becomes one case of it. New materials are wanted for both: stucco, moulded plaster, sheet-metal roofing, oak framing, lime infill, clay tile.
16. **A menu, in play, for the weather, the hour, the mist and the like.** Done. **Weather** in the bar at the foot of the screen opens it: cloud, heaped or in banks, mist, haze and wind change as one watches; a new time of day builds the place again (a few seconds) and puts the player back on the spot where they stood, since the sun's direction is in its shadows, its baked light and the halls' own light. 17. **Can ground materials be better and more realistic now, with WebGPU?** Asked as a question, and then answered by the one who asked: of the work on ground set aside earlier, "it does not need to be deferred anymore". So it is wanted now, after the buildings. Begun (and the dead grass was not in fact drawn until the commit after: its recipe had a brace too many and did not compile, so those patches were drawn dark; mended, looked at again, and toned down in sun): dead grass and moss lie in the turf, the turf is less of a lawn, and the ruts of the track are pressed dark with puddles in their low places (document 4, *Ground*). Still to do: displacement near the eye for turf and earth as well as for stones, mud at the water's edge, grass that is grown and thinned on the graphics card, and ground judged in the baked light, which these pictures were not. Somewhat: the limit of sixteen textures to a material is gone, so ground can blend more kinds of surface than the four it has and keep a normal and a roughness map for each; and work can be done on the graphics card that could not be before (grass grown and thinned per frame, wetness and puddles from a map of where water gathers). But most of what makes ground look real is not the interface: it is better textures, displacement near the eye, and how the kinds meet, and that was set aside earlier for the feature work (see item 10).
18. **Things to see far off: monuments, towers, castles on the hills of the far landscape.** Done as a first pass. 19. **The valley itself: "a silly square quarry with a building blocking the upper valley".     - *Done, the shape.* A valley is no longer a box with a wall of one height round it. ## Risks

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| The target look is not reachable at an acceptable frame rate in a browser | The look spike comes second, before generation depends on rendering decisions; quality tiers; baking instead of per-frame cost |
| Baking light takes too long after each generation | Progressive refinement while the player walks; cheaper storage first; bake per zone and reuse when only dressing is re-rolled |
| Procedural materials look synthetic | Start with a few and make them excellent; keep the path for scanned texture sets open |
| Embedding looped graphs in space fails or produces stretched layouts | Coarse-grid embedding with early rejection; layout-aware cycle patterns; cheap retries |
| Open terrain does not hold the graph: players walk around the structure | Hard and soft edges generated from the graph; a walkability check outdoors as a hard constraint |
| Castles and towns need far more authored content than delves | One kind per milestone; shells for most buildings; a parameterised kit of parts shared across cultures |
| Output is valid but samey | Expressive range analysis from M1 onwards; premise, setting and signature drive differences top-down |
| Metrics are optimised and the result gets worse | Treat metrics as filters for bad output; tune against human judgement of walked sites |
| History passes break completability | Mission edges are protected or become the cause of locks; the solver runs after every mutating layer |
| Scope | Each milestone is a complete slice that can be judged; later kinds of site can be deferred without wasting earlier work |

## Decisions

### Settled

- **Presentation.** A 3D world in the browser, plus 2D maps as assets in their own right.
- **Order.** 3D first. The look is built and proven before the generator.
- **Visual direction.** Physically based materials with normal maps, and lighting treated as a first-class part of generation. Not a low-poly style: geometry is dense, and anything that should catch light on an edge is modelled rather than faked.
- **Range.** Any place of adventure: underground, castles, towns and open country.

### Still open

Defaults are what the plan currently assumes.

1. **Texture sourcing.** Default: procedural materials first, with scanned sets accepted where a recipe falls short. The alternative is to lean on scanned libraries from the start, which looks good sooner but varies less and adds download weight.
2. **Hardware floor.** Default: desktop browsers on a mid-range laptop at 60 frames per second, with a lower tier for integrated graphics. Phones are not targeted.
3. **What the eventual game does.** Default: exploration and puzzles, with threats as abstract placeholders. Combat would add needs to population and to space (arenas, cover).
4. **Camera.** Default: first person, plus a fly-over. Third person would add a character and constrain tight interiors.
5. **Site size.** Default: a 20 to 40 minute site, up to a few hundred metres across. Much larger sites would bring streaming and level-of-detail work forward.
